Day 2 · Portfolio Section 1 — My Financial Values & Goals

NJSLS 9.1.8.FP.5
Today's goalI can articulate my personal financial values and set one short-term and one long-term SMART financial goal, explaining how my values shape each one.

Back in Unit 1, you learned about financial psychology (9.1.8.FP.5): financial decisions are never purely mathematical. They're shaped by values, emotions, habits, and the influence of family and friends. Recognizing this matters because it helps people make decisions they'll actually stick with, instead of ones that look good on paper but ignore what they genuinely care about. A perfectly logical savings plan that ignores what a person actually values tends to fall apart the first time it gets inconvenient.

You also learned the SMART goal framework: a strong goal is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. Here's the classic move from vague to SMART: "I want to save money" says almost nothing useful — it doesn't say how much, by when, or why. Turned into a SMART goal: "I will save $15/month from my part-time job for 6 months to buy a $90 pair of headphones, because I value having quality tools for my hobby (music)." Every piece is checkable: Specific (headphones, $90), Measurable ($15/month tracked), Achievable (fits a part-time income), Relevant (ties to a stated value — music), Time-bound (6 months exactly).

Notice that last example didn't stop at "Time-bound" — it explicitly named a value (caring about music/hobby tools) as the reason the goal exists. That's exactly what Portfolio Section 1 asks you to do: name your top 3 financial values, set one short-term goal (within 6 months) and one long-term goal (1+ years) connected to those values, and explain in 2-3 sentences how your values specifically shaped each goal — not just what the goal is, but why it's genuinely yours.

A quick worked example of the full connection: suppose a student's top values include independence and education. A short-term goal like "save $120 over 3 months toward a used laptop for schoolwork" connects directly to both — it's a step toward independence (owning a tool rather than always borrowing one) and education (better ability to do schoolwork). A long-term goal like "save $2,000 toward a car over the next 3 years" connects to independence even more directly. The values aren't decoration on top of the goals — they're the actual reason the goals were chosen in the first place. Try building your own values and goals in the tool below.

Day 3 · Portfolio Section 2 — My Income, Budget & Spending Plan

NJSLS 9.1.8.PB.4; PB.6
Today's goalI can construct a realistic monthly budget that allocates income across needs, wants, and savings, and explain how I'd adjust it in response to a change in circumstances.

Unit 3 introduced budgeting as developing a plan that aligns spending with your goals and values (9.1.8.PB.4), and adjusting that plan when circumstances change (9.1.8.PB.6). Today is the practical bridge between yesterday's values and goals and real monthly numbers — a budget is where a goal actually turns into a specific dollar amount you commit to setting aside every month.

Quick recap of the structure from Unit 3: every budget line falls into needs (things you can't reasonably go without — phone plan, transportation, essentials), wants (things that make life more enjoyable but aren't essential — entertainment, eating out, extra clothes), or savings (money set aside for a goal or emergency, rather than spent). And a budget should be built on realistic take-home income — what actually lands in your pocket after any withholding, not the larger gross figure, a distinction Units 2 and 7 both covered.

Here's the worked example Section 2 is modeled on, using a starting monthly income of $600.00. Needs: phone plan $30.00, transportation $40.00, personal care/other essentials $20.00 — subtotal $90.00. Wants: entertainment/subscriptions $35.00, eating out $35.00, clothes/other wants $40.00 — subtotal $110.00. Savings: a goal contribution tied to a Section 1 goal, $25.00, plus general/emergency savings $375.00 — subtotal $400.00. Adding it up: $90.00 + $110.00 + $400.00 = $600.00, matching total income exactly. Notice the savings lines connect directly to goals — that's not optional decoration, it's the whole point of Section 2.

The second half of Section 2 is a "Life Happens" scenario — a change in circumstances you have to respond to using your own budget, referencing specific line items by name rather than a vague "I'd spend less." A strong response also recognizes whether a change is temporary (a one-time repair) or permanent (an ongoing bill increase), since a permanent change needs an ongoing fix, not a one-month patch. Day 4 · Portfolio Section 3 — My Banking & Saving Strategy

NJSLS 9.1.8.FI.3
Today's goalI can select and justify a financial institution and account type for my savings plan, and calculate how much my savings would grow over one year.

Unit 4 covered choosing among financial institutions and account types (9.1.8.FI.3): banks, credit unions, and online-only banks each offer different trade-offs in fees, interest rates, and accessibility. Today's updated comparison chart adds real numbers to that trade-off. A traditional bank typically charges $0–$12/month (often waived with direct deposit or a minimum balance), pays a very low savings rate of 0.01%–0.05%, but offers widespread branches and ATMs. A credit union is often $0/month, member-owned, usually requires a small membership deposit, pays a somewhat better 0.05%–0.5% rate, but has fewer branches (though often shares ATM networks) and membership eligibility rules. An online-only bank usually charges no monthly fee at all, pays the highest rate of the three — 0.5%–4.5%, varying with the market — but has no physical branches, relying entirely on an app or phone/chat support.

Interest connects straight back to Section 1's long-term goal: a savings account with a higher interest rate grows a goal's savings faster over time, even with the exact same monthly contribution. Here's the one-year growth formula: Total Contributed (monthly amount × 12) + (Total Contributed × interest rate) = Year-End Total. Worked example: a student saves $50.00/month in an online-only savings account at a 2% annual rate. Total Contributed = $50.00 × 12 = $600.00. Interest Earned = $600.00 × 2% = $12.00. Year-End Total = $600.00 + $12.00 = $612.00. This is simple annual interest for estimation purposes — real accounts often compound more frequently, which would make the actual total slightly higher, but simple interest is close enough to see the pattern clearly at this level.

Section 3 asks you to choose which institution type and account (checking, savings, or both) best fits your Section 2 savings plan, calculate roughly how much your goal would grow in one year at your chosen institution's rate, and justify your choice using at least one specific fee or feature from the comparison chart — not a vague reason like "it seemed fine."

The right choice depends on what a person actually needs. Someone who values in-person help and doesn't mind a lower interest rate might reasonably choose a traditional bank despite its low rate, specifically because of its wide branch network. Someone chasing the fastest possible growth on a long-term goal, and comfortable managing everything through an app, might choose an online-only bank for its much higher rate, accepting the trade-off of no physical branch. Neither choice is automatically "correct" — the justification is what matters, and it has to cite something specific from the chart.

Day 5 · Portfolio Section 4 — My Credit, Civic & Protection Plan

NJSLS 9.1.8.CDM.1 (synth. CR.1-3, RM.1, EG.9, CR.4)
Today's goalI can explain responsible credit use, identify my civic financial responsibilities, and describe personal fraud-protection habits as one unified plan.

Unit 6 covered responsible credit and debt management (9.1.8.CDM.1): understanding how credit works, why paying at least the minimum on time matters, and how to avoid debt traps like the minimum-payment trap and payday loans. Here's the key idea worth restating clearly: paying only the minimum due on a credit card lets interest pile up, turning a small purchase into something that takes months or years — and far more money — to pay off. A responsible credit habit isn't just "pay the minimum," it's "pay more than the minimum whenever possible."

Today connects that idea to two more strands from later in the year. Unit 7's civic financial responsibility (CR.1-3) covers paying taxes and understanding how tax dollars fund shared public services — schools, roads, safety — as part of participating responsibly in a community. Unit 8's risk management and fraud awareness (RM.1, EG.9) covers insurance decisions and protecting personal information from scams. All three — credit, civic responsibility, and fraud protection — are really different dimensions of the same idea: being a financially responsible adult who looks out for their own money and plays their part in a shared system.

A few terms worth having fresh before you write. Credit score: a number reflecting how reliably someone repays borrowed money, which affects the interest rates and loan approvals a person qualifies for. Payroll tax / FICA: money withheld from every paycheck to fund Social Security and Medicare. Risk pooling: insurance works by pooling many people's premiums so the pool can cover the smaller number of people who have a major unplanned loss. Shared consumer protection (9.1.8.CR.4): consumers, businesses, and government each hold distinct responsibilities for preventing and responding to fraud.

Section 4 asks for a three-part personal responsibility plan, written with real specifics rather than vague statements. Part 1 — Credit habit: one specific habit you commit to (or commit to avoiding), using at least one Unit 6 term, and explaining why it matters. Part 2 — Civic/tax understanding: one specific thing you now understand better about taxes or civic responsibility, and why it matters to a community. Part 3 — Fraud-protection/insurance habits: TWO specific habits you plan to practice, each using at least one Unit 8 term. "I'll be responsible with money" is too vague for any of the three parts — name an exact habit, term, and reason.

It asks two things: what is the single most useful thing you learned this year, and how do you expect to actually use it in the next few years; and what is one money question you still have that this class didn't fully answer. Finally, the Grade 7 Cumulative Final Assessment: 8 multiple-choice questions spanning Units 1 through 8, plus one extended-response question. The extended response asks you to write a short letter of advice to a friend who's about to start their first part-time job, covering what they should know about their paycheck and taxes, how they might build a simple budget, one saving or banking decision to think about, and one habit that helps protect their money or information — referencing at least three different units by name.

Before the real assessment, here's a light checkpoint to warm up. And one last thing worth saying plainly: finishing this portfolio does not mean you're "done" learning about values and goals, budgeting, banking, or credit and protection. Grade 8 will revisit every one of these strands again, at a higher level and in new situations — this portfolio is a genuine baseline for where you're starting from, not a final word on any of it.
