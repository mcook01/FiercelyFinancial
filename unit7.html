Day 1 · Taxes & Your Paycheck: Gross vs. Net Income

NJSLS 9.1.8.EG.1
Today's goalI can explain how taxes affect disposable income and describe the difference between gross income and net income.

If a job posting says it pays $15 an hour, does the paycheck you actually receive equal exactly hours worked times $15? It doesn't — and today's lesson is about exactly why, using two numbers that never match: gross income (total earnings before anything is taken out — the number on the job offer, or hours worked times pay rate) and net income, also called take-home pay (what's actually deposited into your account, after taxes and other withholdings are subtracted). Every single paycheck has both numbers, and they are almost never the same.

The gap between them is called withholding: the portion of each paycheck an employer is legally required to hold back and send to the government on your behalf, before you ever see it. Three kinds of withholding make up that gap. Federal income tax is withheld by every employer in the country. State income tax is withheld too, in most states (a few states don't collect one, but most — including New Jersey — do). And payroll tax, often labeled FICA on a pay stub, is withheld specifically to fund Social Security and Medicare — it usually shows up as two separate lines, Social Security and Medicare, even though both fall under the FICA umbrella.

Here's a complete worked example, using a real pay stub. Someone works 80 hours over a two-week (biweekly) pay period at $16.00 an hour. Gross pay: 80 × $16.00 = $1,280.00. Now the withholding, one line at a time. Federal income tax (10%): $1,280.00 × 0.10 = $128.00. State income tax (3%): $1,280.00 × 0.03 = $38.40. Social Security (6.2%): $1,280.00 × 0.062 = $79.36. Medicare (1.45%): $1,280.00 × 0.0145 = $18.56. Add those four withholding lines together: $128.00 + $38.40 + $79.36 + $18.56 = $264.32 in total withholding. Subtract that from gross pay: $1,280.00 − $264.32 = $1,015.68 — the actual net pay deposited into the account. Gross pay and net pay are different numbers precisely because of that $264.32 gap, made up entirely of the four withholding lines above.

Net pay still isn't the number that matters most for budgeting, though — that's disposable income (9.1.8.EG.1): the money left after taxes that a person can actually spend, save, or give, once typical essential expenses are covered too. This is the bridge straight back to Unit 3's budgeting worksheets, where "needs" got covered before anything else. Here's a second worked example showing that last step. Someone's net pay for a period comes to $753.82. Their essential expenses for that same period — a share of housing/utilities ($300.00), phone ($45.00), transportation ($60.00), and food ($150.00) — add up to $555.00. Disposable income = net pay − essential expenses = $753.82 − $555.00 = $198.82. That $198.82 is the number that actually determines what's available for a Unit 3-style savings goal, a want, or a giving contribution — not the $950.00 gross pay this person started with, and not even the $753.82 net pay before expenses were covered.

It's worth being precise about what withholding is for. Federal and state income tax fund the wide range of public services Day 2 digs into in detail — everything from roads to schools to national defense. The Social Security and Medicare withholding is different: that money is set aside specifically to fund retirement benefits and healthcare coverage for older and disabled Americans, including, eventually, the same worker whose paycheck it's withheld from today. None of it disappears into nothing — every withheld dollar is funding something specific, even though it can feel invisible on a random Tuesday paycheck.

Not every deduction on a pay stub is a tax, though, and the difference matters. A paycheck might also show money taken out for health insurance premiums or a retirement contribution (like a 401(k)). Those look similar to tax withholding — they also reduce net pay — but they work completely differently: they're voluntary (an employee usually chooses to enroll), the money isn't sent to the government at all, and it stays connected to that specific employee — a retirement contribution keeps growing in that person's own retirement account, and a health insurance premium buys that person's own coverage. A tax withholding, by contrast, is required by law and funds shared public programs and services rather than something that comes directly back to the one person who paid it.

Understanding this chain — gross income minus withholding equals net income, and net income minus essential expenses equals disposable income — is what prevents an unpleasant surprise the first time a real paycheck arrives. A $15/hour job posting was never going to turn into exactly $15 an hour landing in an account; now you know precisely why, and exactly how to calculate what will actually show up instead. Day 2 · Types of Taxes & How Government Uses Them

NJSLS 9.1.8.EG.2–3; SS 6.3.8.CivicsPI.1
Today's goalI can explain why various sources of income are taxed differently and evaluate how local, state, and federal governments use taxes to fund public services.

Name three things in your town that are paid for with tax dollars, even though no one hands over cash for them directly — a paved road, a working streetlight, a fire truck that shows up when called. None of those are free to provide; they're funded by one of four main types of taxes, each collected differently and each funding something a little different.

Income tax is a percentage of what a person earns, collected by the federal government and, in most states, the state government too — and it's often structured so that different income levels are taxed at different rates, a policy choice tied to ideas about fairness that Day 1's withholding math already showed in action. Sales tax is a percentage added to the price of a purchase at checkout, and critically, the same rate applies to everyone regardless of income — a family ordering a $40 pizza and a family earning ten times as much both pay the identical percentage on that same $40 purchase. Using an example rate of 6%: $40 × 0.06 = $2.40 in sales tax, for a total of $42.40 — and that math comes out exactly the same for every buyer, no matter their income. (Real sales tax rates vary by state.) Property tax is based on the value of real estate someone owns — a home, a store, an apartment building — and it largely funds local schools and services. Payroll tax, already introduced Day 1 as FICA, funds Social Security and Medicare specifically.

That "same rate for everyone" feature is exactly what separates sales tax from income tax, and it's worth sitting with for a second. Income tax can vary by how much a person earns, which is a deliberate government policy choice. Sales tax, by design, does not vary by income at all — it's a flat percentage tied only to the price of what's being bought, not to who's buying it. (Some other kinds of income, like certain investment gains, can be taxed at yet another set of rates — a topic Grade 8 covers in more depth. For now, the key distinction is simply that not all income is taxed identically, and that's a policy choice too.)

Here's exactly how governments use what gets collected. Federal taxes fund things like national defense, Social Security, and federal highways (think interstate highways that cross state lines). State taxes fund things like state highways and public universities. Local taxes — overwhelmingly property tax — fund things like public schools, local police and fire departments, libraries, and parks. Matching a real list of services makes the pattern click fast: public schools (local), interstate highways (federal), a local fire department (local), national parks (federal), Social Security checks (federal), a city library (local), a public university (state), neighborhood road repaving (local), national defense (federal), and the local police department (local). Notice how many everyday, close-to-home services — schools, police, fire, the library, road repaving — are funded locally, mostly through property tax, while the largest, most far-reaching programs — national defense, Social Security, interstate highways — are funded federally.

This connects directly back to Day 1: the income tax and payroll tax withheld from a paycheck don't vanish into an abstract place called "the government." Income tax withholding flows toward the federal (and often state) programs listed above, and payroll tax withholding flows specifically toward Social Security and Medicare. Property tax works differently — it isn't withheld from a paycheck at all, since it's collected directly from whoever owns real estate, which is exactly why a family that rents an apartment still benefits from property-tax-funded schools and services even without paying that tax bill themselves — their landlord factors that cost into the rent.

This is also a good moment to be precise about what today's lesson is, and isn't, about. Understanding how the tax system funds specific public services is factual, civic knowledge (SS 6.3.8.CivicsPI.1) — not a judgment about whether any particular tax rate is too high, too low, or exactly right. Reasonable people disagree about tax policy, and that's a legitimate, ongoing conversation; today's goal is simply understanding how the system currently works, which is the necessary first step before anyone can meaningfully weigh in on that conversation at all.

Put it all together with one more example. A student rides a school bus down a freshly repaved neighborhood street, past a public library, on the way to a public school — three services in one short trip, and all three funded primarily by local property tax. That same student's family might later drive on an interstate highway on a road trip — funded federally — or visit a state university campus on a tour — funded by the state. Nearly everything a household touches in a normal week connects back to one of these four tax types and one of these three levels of government, whether or not anyone ever notices the connection.

Day 3 · Giving Back: Philanthropy, Volunteering & Civic Responsibility

NJSLS 9.1.8.CR.1–2
Today's goalI can compare philanthropy, charity, volunteering, and advocacy, and identify realistic ways to give back based on my own strengths and passions.

Besides donating money, name at least one other way a person could "give back" to their community. If an answer came to mind quickly, today's lesson will put a name to it — because giving back to a community takes several distinct forms, and money is only one of them.

Philanthropy is often larger-scale giving, sometimes through foundations, aimed at long-term community impact — funding a new community center is a classic example. Charity is organizations or giving that address specific, often immediate needs, like food, shelter, or emergency relief. Volunteering means donating time and skills rather than money — tutoring, coaching, cleaning up a park, serving at a food pantry. And advocacy means using your voice to support a cause or push for change — writing letters, raising awareness, organizing petitions. The four overlap in spirit (all of them strengthen a community) but differ in exactly what's being given: philanthropy and charity typically give money or resources at different scales, while volunteering gives time and skill, and advocacy gives voice and attention.

Giving-back traditions exist across many cultures, and while the specific practices differ, they all serve the same underlying purpose: strengthening community and quality of life. Tzedakah is a Jewish tradition in which giving to those in need is considered a moral obligation and an act of justice, not just an optional kindness. Zakat is one of the Five Pillars of Islam — giving a set portion of one's wealth each year to those in need. Mutual aid and "barn-raising" describes a tradition, especially in many rural and farming communities historically, of neighbors pooling labor and resources to help each other complete big tasks. Corporate philanthropy and matching-gift programs are a modern giving tradition where companies donate directly to causes or match the charitable donations their own employees make. And community-centered giving networks, rooted in a range of cultures — including many East Asian communities — emphasize family and community responsibility, expressed through structured mutual-support and volunteer networks. Five very different traditions, five very different specific practices — and every one of them is built around the same core idea: a community is stronger when its members look out for each other on purpose.

The most useful idea from today (9.1.8.CR.2) is this: giving back can be matched to a person's own strengths, passions, and available resources. Nobody needs money to contribute meaningfully — time, skills, and voice all count just as much. A simple "Find Your Fit" framework sorts every give-back option into four categories. Time: tutoring a younger student, walking dogs at an animal shelter. Skills: teaching a friend a skill for free, building a website for a local nonprofit. Money: donating part of an allowance, organizing a fundraiser or supply drive. Voice: posting to raise awareness for a cause, writing to a local official about an issue. Almost anyone has at least one of these four available, even on a week with no spare money at all.

Here's a worked example of matching strengths to a give-back option. Amara has no extra money to give this month, but she's patient, organized, and good at explaining math to people who are stuck. Running her situation through the four categories: money is out for now, but skills and time are both wide open — she could tutor a younger student in math after school (time + skills at once), which costs her nothing financially but genuinely helps someone and uses exactly what she's already good at. Notice that "I don't have money to give" was never actually a dead end here — it just meant looking at the other three categories instead of stopping at the first one.

It's worth directly naming a misconception this lesson often runs into: the idea that civic giving requires money. It doesn't. A person with zero dollars to spare can still volunteer, still use a skill to help someone, and still advocate for a cause they care about — all three are complete, legitimate ways to give back, not lesser substitutes for a cash donation. The goal isn't ranking these traditions or options against each other; it's recognizing that "giving back" is bigger and more flexible than just writing a check, and that everyone has at least one realistic way in. Day 4 picks this idea back up directly, when a Community Giving-Back Fund becomes one of the categories in this unit's culminating budget task — a reminder that even government-level budgeting treats giving back as a real, ongoing priority, not an afterthought.

Day 4 · Unit Review & Assessment: Community Budget Simulation

Synthesis: EG.1–3; CR.1–3; SS 6.3.8
Today's goalI can demonstrate understanding of taxes, disposable income, public funding, and civic responsibility by allocating a simplified local budget and justifying my choices.

Three days ago you started with a single paycheck and the gap between gross and net pay. Since then you've learned which type of tax funds which level of government (Day 2), and seen that giving back doesn't require money at all (Day 3). Today pulls all three days together into one synthesis idea, and one culminating task: a real community budget.

That synthesis idea is 9.1.8.CR.3: consumers, businesses, and government all share responsibility for a healthy economy and community. Consumers hold up their share through everyday spending choices and civic participation — the same disposable-income decisions from Day 1, plus things like voting and staying informed. Businesses hold up their share through fair practices and local investment — paying workers fairly, following the law, and reinvesting in the community they operate in. Government holds up its share by using tax dollars wisely on shared community priorities — exactly what Day 2's federal/state/local funding map showed in action. None of the three carries the whole load alone; a healthy community depends on all three doing their part.

Two misconceptions are worth clearing up directly before the performance task below. First: taxes don't just fund "government" in some abstract sense — they fund the specific, everyday services from Day 2's list: the school bus route, the library, the repaved street, the fire department that shows up when called. Second: civic giving doesn't require money, as Day 3 showed clearly — time, skills, and voice are just as real a contribution as a cash donation.

Here's the task. A town collected $500,000 in local tax revenue this year. As the town's budget committee, your job is to allocate the full amount across five categories: Public Schools, Roads & Infrastructure, Parks & Recreation, Public Safety (Police/Fire), and a Community Giving-Back Fund. Two rules: every category must receive at least $50,000, and your allocations must add up to exactly $500,000. There is no single "correct" split — a budget gets evaluated on the reasoning behind it, not on hitting one specific number.

Here's a fully worked example allocation, to model exactly what a strong answer looks like. Schools: $200,000. Roads & Infrastructure: $120,000. Parks & Recreation: $60,000. Public Safety: $90,000. Community Giving-Back Fund: $30,000. Check the total: $200,000 + $120,000 + $60,000 + $90,000 + $30,000 = $500,000 exactly, and every category clears the $50,000 minimum. A justification for this split might read: "Schools got the largest share because they're a service every family in town relies on directly, largely funded by the same local property tax that pays this budget. Public Safety came next because police and fire protection are core local responsibility, funded the same way. The Community Giving-Back Fund stayed at the $50,000-plus minimum rather than zero, because government sharing responsibility for the community (CR.3) means treating civic giving as a real budget priority, not an afterthought — the same idea Day 3 covered. Meanwhile, residents and local businesses still carry their own share of community responsibility through the spending, hiring, and civic participation choices this budget alone can't cover." Notice what that justification does: it references at least two specific public services, connects them to a specific tax type or funding level from Day 2, and explicitly addresses how consumers, businesses, and government all share responsibility — exactly what today's task and official assessment ask for.
