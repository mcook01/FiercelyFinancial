Day 1 · Predicting Expenses: What Will You Really Spend On?

NJSLS 9.1.8.PB.1–2
Today's goalI can predict the fixed, variable, and periodic expenses that belong in a personal budget and explain how life circumstances change what a realistic budget includes.

A budget is a plan for how money coming in (your income) will be spent, saved, and given over a period of time — usually a month. A budget isn't a punishment or a way to stop you from having fun; it's simply a plan, written down ahead of time, so your money goes where you actually want it to go instead of quietly disappearing. Before you can build a real budget — that starts tomorrow, on Day 2 — you first need to get good at spotting everything that actually belongs in one. And that starts with understanding that expenses don't all behave the same way. They show up in three different timing patterns: fixed, variable, and periodic.

Fixed expenses are the easiest to predict: they cost the same amount, on a regular schedule, no matter what. Rent is the classic example — if rent is $900 a month, it's $900 every single month, whether the apartment gets used a little or a lot. A cell phone plan, a streaming subscription, and a monthly gym membership are usually fixed too. Because fixed expenses don't change, they're the simplest ones to plan for — you already know exactly what to set aside.

Variable expenses are still regular — they show up often — but the amount changes each time. Groceries are the go-to example: you buy groceries every week, but you might spend $60 one week and $85 the next, depending on what's on sale or what you're cooking. Gas for a car, eating out, and electricity bills (which often creep up in summer or winter) are variable for the same reason: the bill always exists, but the number on it moves around.

Periodic expenses are the trickiest, because they're the ones most likely to catch a budget off guard. They're occasional — they don't show up every month — but they are predictable, because you know roughly when they're coming. An annual car registration fee, a yearly membership renewal, holiday or birthday gifts, and back-to-school supplies each fall are all periodic: nothing in July, but a real cost in October. Here's why that matters so much. Imagine a $120 car registration fee that comes due every October. If nobody planned for it, October's budget suddenly has a $120 hole in it that feels like a "surprise" — except it isn't a surprise at all, because it happens on the same predictable schedule every single year. The fix is simple math: $120 ÷ 12 months = $10 a month. Set aside just $10 a month, all year long, and the October fee is already sitting there waiting, covered, calmly, with zero drama. That's the entire point of recognizing a periodic expense ahead of time — it turns a once-a-year shock into a small, steady, boring monthly habit. Boring is good when it comes to money.

Missing a periodic expense is one of the most common ways real people — not just middle schoolers — get blindsided by a cost that was actually predictable the entire time. Learning to spot fixed, variable, and periodic expenses now means you'll rarely be caught flat-footed by one later.

Here's the second half of today's lesson: what belongs in a budget isn't the same for every person, because life circumstances change what expenses exist in the first place. Picture two people. A 16-year-old who lives with their parents usually has housing, groceries, and utilities covered by someone else — their own budget might only need to cover entertainment and personal spending money. Now picture a 22-year-old living alone in their own apartment with a car payment: suddenly rent, utilities, groceries, transportation, and everything else lands on one person's budget, all at once. Neither budget is "wrong" — both are realistic for the situation they're built for. The 16-year-old isn't better at budgeting because their list is shorter; the list is shorter because fewer expenses currently belong to them.

Location changes a budget too. Someone living in a rural area with no public transportation may genuinely need a car — gas, insurance, and registration become required expenses, not optional ones. Someone living in a city with reliable buses or trains might choose not to own a car at all, trading a car payment for a much smaller weekly fare. Family size matters as well: a household feeding five people has a bigger grocery line than someone living alone, even though "groceries" is the exact same category in both budgets.

It helps to separate periodic expenses from a fourth kind of cost that a budget can't fully predict: an unplanned or emergency expense, like a phone screen cracking or a sudden trip to the doctor. A periodic expense repeats on a knowable schedule, so it belongs in the budget's regular categories, spread out ahead of time, the same way the registration fee did above. An emergency expense doesn't have a schedule at all — nobody can say in advance exactly when a phone will crack. That's precisely why some budgets set aside a small monthly cushion, sometimes called an emergency fund, specifically for costs that can't be predicted the way rent or an annual fee can. You'll build more on this idea in a later unit; for now, just notice that "periodic" and "emergency" aren't two names for the same thing, even though both can feel like a surprise if nobody planned ahead.

One more worked example, this time with an expense that repeats more than once a year. Suppose a student plays a school sport with a $60 participation fee due twice a year — once for the fall season and once for the spring season. Treated correctly as periodic: $60 × 2 = $120 a year, and $120 ÷ 12 months = $10 a month set aside, all year long, so both fee due-dates are already covered when they arrive. Treated incorrectly — ignored until the week it's due — that same $60 becomes a scramble twice a year instead of a small, steady $10-a-month habit. The math doesn't change; only whether someone planned for it does.

Today is intentionally about prediction and awareness, not math — the real budget-building starts tomorrow, on Day 2. But hang onto today's categories: fixed, variable, periodic, needs, and wants are exactly what you'll be plugging real numbers into next.

Day 2 · Building Your First Budget

NJSLS 9.1.8.PB.3–4
Today's goalI can construct a simple personal budget from an income and expense categories that aligns with a financial goal.

Time to actually build one. A budget comes together in four steps, in this exact order: (1) list your income, (2) list and total your expenses by category — needs first, then wants, then savings/goal, (3) subtract your total expenses from your income, and (4) adjust if spending is more than income. That's the whole process. Every budget you'll ever build, for the rest of your life, is some version of those same four steps.

Notice where savings sits in that list: it's part of step 2, grouped in right alongside needs and wants — not an afterthought squeezed in at the end if anything happens to be left over. A budget that plans for savings the same way it plans for rent or groceries is far more likely to actually reach a goal than one that just hopes there'll be money left at the end of the month. Treat your future goal like a bill you owe yourself.

Let's walk through the whole four-step process with real numbers, so you can see exactly how it works before you build your own below. Imagine a student earning $100 a month total — $60 from a weekly allowance and $40 from babysitting.

Step 1 — List income: $100/month. That's the entire amount available to plan with; nothing gets spent before it's written down here first.

Step 2 — List and total expenses by category, needs first:

Needs: a $10/month add-on to stay on the family phone plan. Needs total: $10.
Wants: $20 entertainment, $15 snacks, $15 clothes. Wants total: $20 + $15 + $15 = $50.
Savings/goal: $30/month toward a $180 goal, which the math works out to reaching in 6 months (180 ÷ 30 = 6). Savings total: $30.
Add all three categories together: $10 + $50 + $30 = $90 total planned spending.

Step 3 — Subtract total expenses from income: $100 income − $90 total expenses = $10 left over.

Step 4 — Adjust: Because income is more than expenses, this budget already balances — there's nothing urgent to fix. That leftover $10 is a real choice, not a problem: it could go straight into the savings goal (reaching $180 in a little under 5 months instead of 6), get split between savings and a want, or just sit as a small cushion in case a category runs a little over one month. If instead the numbers had come out negative — say expenses added up to $110 against $100 income — step 4 would mean going back and lowering a want (not a need) until the budget balanced again, since needs shouldn't be the first thing cut.

This connects directly back to earlier units: needs come before wants before goals (that's Unit 1), and Unit 2's income types — a wage, an allowance, gig income — are exactly what fills in step 1 of this process. Whatever income sources you identified back then are the real starting number for the budget you're about to build below, using your own numbers instead of the example above.

A lot of adults use a rule of thumb to sanity-check a budget once it's built: roughly 50% toward needs, 30% toward wants, and 20% toward savings and goals combined. It's not a law — the example above had needs at far less than 50%, since a 7th grader's needs list is usually shorter than an adult's paying full rent and utilities — but the rule is still a useful rough check. If wants are eating up 70% of a budget and savings is stuck at 2%, that's a signal worth noticing, even when the exact numbers don't matter as much as the direction.

Let's run the four steps through a second example with different numbers, so the process feels like a tool that works with any income, not just one memorized case. Say a student earns $75 a month from allowance alone, with a goal to save $150 for a new pair of cleats.

Step 1: Income = $75/month.
Step 2: Needs: $5 (a small contribution to a shared family streaming cost). Wants: $10 games + $10 snacks + $5 one movie = $25. Savings: a $150 goal reached in 5 months means $150 ÷ 5 = $30/month. Total planned: $5 + $25 + $30 = $60.
Step 3: $75 income − $60 total expenses = $15 left over.
Step 4: Since income covers expenses with room to spare, this budget balances on the very first try — that extra $15 could shorten the timeline on the cleats to under 4 months, or simply become a small cushion.

Two mistakes are worth watching for as you build your own budget below. The first is forgetting a category entirely — especially a periodic expense from Day 1 that only shows up once or twice a year, since it's easy to leave off a list built in the moment. The second is listing a want at a size that's really a wish rather than a real number — writing down $5 for "snacks" when actual spending has been closer to $20 doesn't make a budget more accurate, it just makes it wrong in a way that shows up later. A budget only works as a plan if the numbers inside it are honest ones.

Day 3 · Comparison Shopping & Smart Spending Strategies

NJSLS 9.1.8.CP.1 & PB.7
Today's goalI can compare total costs across sellers and describe strategies — comparison shopping, negotiating, and everyday habits — that lower what I spend.

Comparison shopping means checking price, quality, and total value across more than one seller before buying. The key word is total: the lowest sticker price isn't always the best value once shipping and tax get added in. A $15 item with $5 shipping costs more, in total, than an $18 item with free shipping — even though $15 looks smaller at first glance. Real comparisons usually involve a third piece too: tax, which most places charge as a percentage of the price.

Here's a complete worked example, using a backpack priced differently at three sellers, with New Jersey's 6.625% sales tax factored in wherever it applies.

Big-Box Store: $25.00 price, $0 shipping (in-store pickup), plus 6.625% sales tax.
Tax owed: $25.00 × 0.06625 = $1.66 (rounded to the nearest cent).
Total cost: $25.00 + $1.66 = $26.66.

Online Marketplace: $19.99 price, $4.99 shipping, plus 6.625% sales tax on the item price.
Tax owed: $19.99 × 0.06625 = $1.32 (rounded).
Item total with tax: $19.99 + $1.32 = $21.31.
Add shipping: $21.31 + $4.99 = $26.30.

Local Shop: $28.00 flat price, $0 shipping, no sales tax charged this weekend — and it comes with a lifetime warranty included.
Total cost: $28.00 (no additional math needed — there's no tax or shipping to add).

Lined up, the totals are Online $26.30, Big-Box $26.66, and Local $28.00. By total cost alone, the Online Marketplace wins — but only by 36 cents over the Big-Box Store, which is close enough that other factors matter. And the Local Shop, even though it's the most expensive by $1.70, throws in a lifetime warranty — meaning if the backpack ever rips or breaks, it gets replaced for free, potentially saving far more than $1.70 down the road. This is exactly what "checking quality and total value, not just price" means in practice: the numerically cheapest option isn't automatically the smartest choice once you weigh in what else you're getting for the money.

Comparison shopping is one of three techniques that can lower what you actually spend. The second is negotiating — politely asking for a lower price or for extras to be included. It works more often than most people expect, especially on discounted floor-model items with small cosmetic damage, at a yard sale, or when buying more than one of something. A simple, respectful script works better than you'd think: "I noticed this has a small scratch — would you be able to offer a discount because of that?" or "I'm interested in this, but I was hoping to pay around $___. Would that work?" or "If I bought two of these, would you be able to do a better price?" Politely asking costs nothing, and the worst possible answer is just "no" — which leaves you exactly where you started.

The third technique is day-to-day expense management: small, repeatable habits that quietly reduce spending over time without ever requiring a negotiation. Bringing lunch from home instead of buying it, shopping with a list so you don't grab extras on impulse, waiting a few days for something to go on sale, and deciding on a spending limit before you even walk into a store all fall into this category.

There's a fourth tool worth knowing alongside comparison shopping, negotiating, and habit awareness: unit price — the cost per single unit of something, like per ounce, per sheet, or per item. Unit price is what makes it possible to compare a big package to a small one fairly. A 12-pack of markers for $6 costs $0.50 each; a 30-pack for $12 costs $0.40 each — even though the 30-pack costs more money up front, each individual marker is actually cheaper. Many stores post unit price right on the shelf tag, below the regular price in smaller print — it's worth a glance before assuming "bigger box always costs more" or "smaller box is always the better deal." Neither guess is reliable without checking.

Quality and reviews matter just as much as price once total cost is figured out. A cheap item that breaks in a week and needs replacing isn't actually cheap once you count the cost of buying it twice. Before buying something that isn't cheap or throwaway — shoes, headphones, a backpack — a quick check of star ratings or a couple of written reviews can catch a problem before it becomes an expensive mistake. This connects right back to the backpack example above: the Local Shop's lifetime warranty was a quality signal every bit as real as its price tag, just a different kind of value.

Here's a second worked example, this time with unit price front and center. Suppose two bottles of the same shampoo sit on a shelf: a 12-ounce bottle for $3.60, and a 20-ounce bottle for $5.20. Unit price for the small bottle: $3.60 ÷ 12 = $0.30 per ounce. Unit price for the large bottle: $5.20 ÷ 20 = $0.26 per ounce. The larger bottle costs more overall ($5.20 vs. $3.60) but is actually the better per-ounce value — a savings of 4 cents an ounce adds up fast if it's a product bought again and again. That's exactly the kind of comparison a total price tag alone would completely miss.

None of this is about being pushy, distrustful, or refusing to spend money at all. It's about making sure that the money you do spend actually gets you the best real value for it — total cost, quality, and all.

Day 4 · Spending Habits & the Power to Save

NJSLS 9.1.8.CP.2
Today's goalI can explain how everyday spending habits affect a person's ability to save money.

Small, frequent purchases add up over time — and can meaningfully reduce how much a person is able to save, even when each individual purchase feels tiny in the moment. Today's skill is a simple three-step calculation that turns any small habit into its real weekly, monthly, and yearly cost, so you can see the whole picture instead of just one purchase at a time.

Here's the full worked example, step by step. Say a student buys a $4 snack after school, three times a week.

Step 1 — Find the weekly cost: cost per purchase × times per week.
$4 × 3 = $12 a week.

Step 2 — Find the monthly cost: weekly cost × about 4.3 weeks in a month (a month is usually a little longer than 4 weeks, so 4.3 is the more accurate multiplier than just 4).
$12 × 4.3 = about $52 a month.

Step 3 — Find the yearly cost: the most reliable way is weekly cost × the number of weeks in a year (52), since that avoids any rounding error from Step 2.
$12 × 52 = $624 a year.

Read that last number again: $624, from a snack that only ever cost $4 at a time. Not one single purchase in that whole year felt like a big decision — each one was just $4. But repeated 156 times (3 times a week × 52 weeks), it becomes a genuinely significant chunk of money that could instead have funded a big chunk of a savings goal, like the $180 or $210 goals from Day 2's budget scenarios.

Frequency matters just as much as size — sometimes more. Compare two different habits: a $3 item bought every single day versus a $15 item bought once a week. At first glance the $15 purchase looks like the bigger splurge. But run the same math: the daily $3 habit is $3 × 7 days = $21 a week, and $21 × 52 = $1,092 a year. The weekly $15 habit is $15 × 52 = $780 a year. The "smaller," daily $3 habit actually costs $312 more per year than the "bigger," weekly $15 one — because it repeats so much more often. That's the whole lesson in one comparison: it's not the size of a single purchase that determines its real impact, it's the size multiplied by how often it happens.

This idea has a well-known nickname: some financial writers call it the "latte factor," named after the idea that a daily coffee purchase, multiplied out over a year, adds up to a surprisingly large number. The name isn't really about coffee specifically — it's shorthand for any small, repeated purchase that's easy to overlook precisely because no single instance of it ever feels expensive.

Subscriptions are a modern version of the same pattern, with a twist: they charge automatically, whether or not they actually get used that month. A $9/month app or streaming subscription is $108 a year — and unlike a snack, it keeps charging even during a month it never gets opened. Checking a bank or account statement every so often for a subscription that quietly kept renewing after interest faded is its own kind of habit awareness — the same three-step math applies, just aimed at a bill instead of a purchase.

There's also a useful way to think about what a redirected habit could become instead, called opportunity cost — what you give up by choosing one use of money over another. Go back to the $624-a-year snack habit from above. That same $624, redirected into a $300 savings goal like the laptop example coming up in Day 5, wouldn't just cover it — it would cover it more than twice over, with money left for something else entirely. That's not a reason to feel bad about a snack; it's just useful information sitting right there, ready to inform an actual choice.

One more worked comparison, using two habits at once. Suppose a student spends $2 on a vending-machine drink most school days (about 5 times a week) and also pays for a $9/month subscription. Drink: $2 × 5 = $10/week, × 52 = $520/year. Subscription: $9 × 12 = $108/year. Combined: $520 + $108 = $628/year — remarkably close to the original snack example, just made of two smaller habits instead of one.

None of this is about guilt, and it's not a call to stop buying anything you enjoy. Some small recurring purchases are a perfectly reasonable use of money — a snack after school, a weekly hangout with friends, a monthly subscription you genuinely use. The goal is informed choice, not deprivation. Once you can see a habit's real weekly, monthly, and yearly cost using the same three steps above, you get to decide on purpose whether it's worth it to you — instead of never noticing the number at all.

Day 5 · Budgeting for Goals: Short-Term, Long-Term & Giving

NJSLS 9.1.8.PB.6
Today's goalI can construct a budget that allocates savings toward a short-term goal, a long-term goal, and a giving goal.

Up to now, your budget has had one single savings/goal line. Today that one line splits into three, because "savings" isn't really one thing — it's three different kinds of goals that each work a little differently.

A short-term goal is achievable in under about 6 months — something like a video game, a concert ticket, or a small gift for someone. It's close enough that you can picture reaching it soon, which makes it easier to stay motivated. A long-term goal takes 6 months or more to reach — a laptop, a big trip, or eventually a car down payment. Long-term goals take patience precisely because the finish line is further away, but the four-step budgeting process from Day 2 works exactly the same way for a long-term goal as a short-term one: set an amount aside every month, and let it add up. A charitable/giving goal means setting aside money, time, or resources on purpose for a cause or person you care about — big or small amounts both count, and giving is exactly as real a budget category as the other two.

Here's a complete worked example of splitting one goal line into three, using the $30/month savings amount from Day 2's worked example (a student earning $100/month, with $10 in needs and $50 in wants, leaving $40 available for everything else — savings and giving combined).

Short-term: $10/month toward a $60 video game. $60 ÷ $10 = 6 months to reach it — comfortably short-term.
Long-term: $25/month toward a $300 laptop. $300 ÷ $25 = 12 months, a full year — solidly long-term, and worth starting now specifically because it takes a while.
Giving: $5/month set aside for a cause the student cares about, or to help out a family member when needed.
Add the three together: $10 + $25 + $5 = $40/month, which matches exactly what was available. Nothing left over, nothing overspent — the three goals fit the budget precisely.

Notice the trade-off baked into that example: reaching a $300 laptop in 12 months instead of 6 months meant giving the long-term goal less per month, which freed up room for the short-term game and the giving amount to fit in the very same budget. If the student instead wanted the laptop in just 6 months, long-term would need $50/month by itself — more than the entire $40 available — so something else would have to shrink or wait. This is exactly what "priorities can shift" means in practice: there's rarely one right split, only trade-offs that match what matters most to you right now.

There's no single "correct" split among the three goal types — but if you're stuck deciding, one reasonable starting point is leaning the biggest share toward whichever goal is closest to being reached, since a nearly-finished goal is often the easiest one to stay motivated about. Once that goal is crossed off, the amount that used to go toward it can shift straight into the next one, without ever changing your total savings amount at all.

Here's a second worked example, with different numbers, to show the same three-way split working for a different income and different goals. Suppose a student earns $60 a month, with $8 in needs and $22 in wants, leaving $30 for savings and giving combined. Short-term: $12/month toward a $48 pair of headphones (48 ÷ 12 = 4 months). Long-term: $15/month toward a $180 bike (180 ÷ 15 = 12 months). Giving: $3/month toward a family member's birthday fund. Check the total: $12 + $15 + $3 = $30, matching exactly what was available — the same balancing check from Day 2's step 3, just applied to three goals instead of one.

Goals can also be accelerated on purpose, temporarily, without blowing up the whole budget. If that same student decided the headphones mattered enough to want them in 2 months instead of 4, bumping short-term from $12 to $24/month would do it — but $24 + $15 + $3 = $42, which is $12 more than the $30 available. Step 4 from Day 2 kicks back in here: either a want gets trimmed to free up that $12, or long-term and giving both pause for a couple of months while short-term gets the extra attention. Neither choice is wrong; it's simply a decision about what matters most right now, made on purpose instead of by accident.

A well-built budget can work toward more than one goal type at once, even in fairly small amounts, and priorities can shift over time as your life and goals change — that's expected and healthy, not a sign anything went wrong. Today you'll revisit the budget you built on Day 2 and split its single savings/goal line into all three types, the same way the example above did.

One note on giving specifically: it's personal, and there's no required cause. It's completely fine to frame it as "giving to someone I know" — a family member, a friend going through a hard time — instead of a formal charity. Amounts should stay small and realistic; a few dollars a month is a completely valid, complete answer.

Day 6 · Unit Review & Assessment: Build-a-Budget

Synthesis: PB.1–4, PB.6–7; CP.1–2
Today's goalI can build a complete budget from a scenario that uses everything from this unit — predicting expenses, smart spending, and saving toward multiple goals.

Six days ago you started by predicting what belongs in a budget. Since then you've built a full budget from real income (Day 2), learned to compare total costs and negotiate (Day 3), seen how small habits add up (Day 4), and split savings across short-term, long-term, and giving goals (Day 5). Today's job is to pull all five of those skills together into one complete scenario — exactly what the official check-in will ask you to do.

Let's walk through what that looks like, end to end, using one running example. Suppose a student earns $100 a month from a part-time job and gift money.

Predicting expenses (Day 1): before assigning a single dollar, the student lists what actually needs covering — a fixed phone add-on, variable snack and entertainment spending, and no periodic expenses this particular month (though a future birthday gift is worth keeping in mind).

Building the budget (Day 2): Needs: $10 (phone add-on). Wants: $50 (entertainment $20 + snacks $15 + clothes $15). That's $60 committed, leaving $40 for savings and giving — step 3 of the process (income $100 − expenses so far $60 = $40 remaining to assign).

Smart spending (Day 3): when it's time to actually buy the clothes or entertainment items in that budget, the student checks total cost — price plus shipping plus tax — across more than one seller, and isn't afraid to politely ask about a discount on a floor-model or multi-item purchase.

Habit awareness (Day 4): before locking in the $15/month snack budget, the student checks its yearly cost: at roughly $15 × 12 = $180 a year, that's real money — worth keeping, but worth knowing the true size of.

Multiple goals (Day 5): the remaining $40 splits into short-term $10 (a $60 goal reached in 6 months), long-term $25 (a $300 goal reached in 12 months), and giving $5 (set aside monthly for a cause the student cares about) — adding back up to exactly $40, so the full $100 income is accounted for with nothing overspent.

Two mix-ups worth clearing up before this unit wraps. First, needs/wants is a different question from fixed/variable/periodic — they're two separate ways of describing the same expense, not two names for the same idea. A want can still be a fixed monthly cost (a streaming subscription is a want, but it's fixed — same price every month). A need can still be variable (groceries are a need, but the amount changes week to week). Don't assume "need" always means "fixed" or "want" always means "variable" — check each expense on both questions separately. Second, savings should be planned for like any other required expense in step 2 of the budgeting process, not treated as whatever happens to be left over after everything else. A budget that plans savings on purpose is dramatically more likely to actually reach its goal than one that hopes there's money left at the end.

Across the unit, most budgeting mistakes trace back to skipping one of the five days' skills rather than getting the math wrong. Forgetting Day 1's periodic expenses turns a predictable cost into a fake emergency. Skipping Day 2's step 4 lets a budget quietly go negative without ever getting fixed. Buying the first option seen instead of running Day 3's total-cost comparison means paying more than necessary for the exact same item. Ignoring Day 4's frequency math lets a "tiny" habit quietly eat a real chunk of a paycheck unnoticed. And treating Day 5's goals as optional extras, instead of planned categories, is how a savings goal never actually gets funded. Every one of these is fixable — and now you have the tools to fix all five.

Before finalizing any budget — today's, or one you build in real life later — it helps to run it through five quick questions, one for each day of this unit. Have I predicted every fixed, variable, and periodic expense that actually applies to my situation (Day 1)? Does my total planned spending, including savings, add up to no more than my income (Day 2)? Have I checked total cost — not just sticker price — before making a purchase in this budget (Day 3)? Have I checked whether any small recurring purchase is costing more per year than it seems to in the moment (Day 4)? And have I planned savings across more than one goal type on purpose, rather than leaving it as whatever's left over (Day 5)? A budget that can answer "yes" to all five has genuinely used everything this unit taught.

A budget also isn't a document you write once and never touch again. Income changes — a raise, a lost babysitting client, a new job. Expenses change too — a periodic fee gets added, a subscription gets canceled. Revisiting a budget every so often and adjusting it, the same way step 4 from Day 2 already showed, is a normal, healthy part of using one — not a sign that the original plan failed.
