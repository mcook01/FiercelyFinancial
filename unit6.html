Day 1 · Credit vs. Debit: Understanding the Difference

NJSLS 9.1.8.CDM.1
Today's goalI can compare credit cards and debit cards for specific purchases and identify at least one advantage and one disadvantage of each.

Credit means borrowing money now with a promise to pay it back later — usually with interest if you don't pay it back in full. Debit means spending money you already have sitting in a bank account, right now, with no borrowing involved at all. That one difference — whose money you're actually spending — is the root of everything else about these two kinds of cards.

A debit card pulls directly from your own checking account balance the moment you swipe it. If there's $40 in the account and you spend $15 on lunch, the account instantly drops to $25 — that's it, no bill arrives later. A credit card works completely differently: the card issuer (a bank or credit card company) fronts the money for you, up to a set credit limit, and then sends you a bill — usually once a month — for whatever you spent. Pay that bill in full, and you owe nothing extra. Pay only part of it, and the rest starts collecting interest, which is exactly what Day 2 digs into in detail.

Because debit never involves borrowing, it carries zero interest risk — you can't get charged interest on money that was already yours. But debit has a real downside if something goes wrong: if a thief gets your debit card number and drains your checking account, that money is gone from your account immediately while the bank investigates, which can leave you short on cash for groceries or bills in the meantime. Credit cards usually offer stronger protection here, because it's the bank's money at risk in the moment, not yours — many credit card issuers will pause a disputed charge and investigate before you're ever required to pay it. That's the trade: debit is simple and risk-free on interest, but credit often protects you better if your card information gets stolen.

Fees are another difference worth knowing about, since neither card is automatically free to use. A credit card can carry a late fee — a flat charge added if a payment doesn't arrive by the due date — and some (though not all) charge an annual fee just for having the card open, whether or not it's ever used. Debit cards can also come with fees, most commonly an overdraft fee if a purchase is made for more money than is actually sitting in the account, which some banks allow to go through anyway and then charge for. Reading the fine print before opening either kind of account — what it costs, and under what conditions — is a habit worth starting now, long before the dollar amounts get bigger.

Credit also does something debit can't: it can help build a credit history — a track record of borrowing and repaying money responsibly that other lenders can look up later (Day 4 covers exactly how this becomes a credit score). Every time a credit card payment is made on time, that gets reported and added to that history. A debit card never builds this kind of record, no matter how responsibly it's used, because there's never actually any borrowing happening. That doesn't make debit "worse" for building good habits, though — using a debit card responsibly still builds real budgeting skill, like tracking a balance and not spending past what's actually available. It just doesn't get reported anywhere the way credit activity does.

Here's the catch that makes credit riskier: because a credit card lets you spend money you don't currently have, it's easy to spend more than you can actually pay back. If that happens, you carry a balance, get charged interest on it, and can end up owing far more than you originally spent. A debit card makes that particular mistake basically impossible, since you're limited to whatever's actually sitting in the account.

So which card is "better"? Neither one, always — it depends on the situation. A $4 coffee you can easily afford is a perfect debit purchase: there's no reason to borrow money for something you already have the cash for. But renting a car is a case where many rental counters actually require a credit card, not a debit card, to hold the reservation. A big online purchase might call for a credit card's stronger fraud protection. And if overspending this month is a worry, debit's built-in limit — you literally cannot spend money you don't have — can be the safer choice.

A simple three-question checklist can make this decision automatic instead of confusing. First: Is the money already sitting in my account? If not, credit is the only option that even works, and it needs to be paid back deliberately. Second: Could something go wrong with this purchase — a scam, a broken item, a company that never ships it? If the stakes are higher, credit's stronger dispute protection is worth leaning on. Third: Am I confident I can pay this off in full, soon? If the honest answer is no, that's usually a sign to slow down and use debit instead, or wait and save up. Running through those three questions takes seconds, and it's exactly the kind of thinking a responsible adult does automatically before every larger purchase.

Try applying all of this to two real people. Priya wants to start building a credit history early, so she uses a credit card only for small, easy-to-afford purchases — like that $4 coffee — and pays the full balance every single month. She never carries a balance, so she never pays a cent of interest, and every on-time payment quietly builds her credit history in the background. Her friend Marcus uses a credit card for a $600 impulse purchase he can't actually afford right now. He can only manage the minimum payment, so the balance sits there collecting interest month after month — turning a $600 purchase into something that costs him considerably more over time, as Day 2 shows in exact numbers. Run Marcus's decision back through the three-question checklist above, and the warning sign was there from the start: he couldn't honestly answer "yes" to paying it off soon. Same tool, two very different outcomes — the card itself isn't the problem or the solution; how it's used is.

Day 2 · How Credit Cards Work: Interest, Fees & the True Cost of Borrowing

NJSLS 9.1.8.CDM.2
Today's goalI can explain credit card terminology and calculate the cost of carrying a balance at a given interest rate.

A handful of terms explain how a credit card actually costs money. APR (annual percentage rate) is the yearly cost of borrowing, charged monthly on any unpaid balance. Minimum payment is the smallest amount required to avoid a late fee — usually a small fraction of the balance. Credit limit is the maximum you're allowed to borrow. Grace period is the time you have to pay your statement balance in full before any interest gets charged. Statement balance is simply what you owed at the end of the billing cycle.

Here's the trap: if you carry a balance — meaning you don't pay your statement balance off in full by the due date — interest gets charged on whatever's left unpaid, so a large share of every payment goes toward interest instead of actually reducing what you owe. That's why paying only the minimum can take years and end up costing far more than the original purchase ever did.

Let's walk through the math with real numbers, so this isn't just a claim — you can see it happen. Say there's a $300 balance on a credit card with a 20% APR. To find the monthly interest rate, divide the yearly rate by 12: 20% ÷ 12 ≈ 1.67% per month. Now suppose the minimum payment is a fixed $25 every month.

Month 1: Interest charged = $300 × 1.67% = $5.01. That gets added to the balance: $300 + $5.01 = $305.01. Then the $25 payment gets subtracted: $305.01 − $25 = $280.01 — the new balance heading into month 2.

Month 2: Interest charged = $280.01 × 1.67% ≈ $4.68. New balance before payment: $280.01 + $4.68 = $284.69. Subtract the $25 payment: $284.69 − $25 = $259.69.

Month 3: Interest charged = $259.69 × 1.67% ≈ $4.34. New balance before payment: $259.69 + $4.34 = $264.03. Subtract the $25 payment: $264.03 − $25 = $239.03.

Notice what's happening: even though $25 gets paid every single month, the balance only drops by about $20 each time — because roughly $4 to $5 of every payment is being eaten by interest instead of shrinking what's owed. Keep that same pattern going for six months total, and the balance only falls to about $174.94 — meaning $150 was paid in, but only around $125 of the original $300 actually got paid off. The other roughly $25 went straight to interest, and the balance still isn't close to zero.

That's the trap in a nutshell: a small monthly payment can feel responsible, but if it's barely bigger than the interest being charged, the balance shrinks agonizingly slowly — and if a payment ever fails to cover even that month's interest, the balance stops shrinking at all and can actually start growing. It's also worth knowing that credit card companies calculate the minimum payment, and they set it low on purpose — a low minimum keeps a balance outstanding for longer, which means more months of interest charges for the company. That's not a conspiracy, just a business fact worth knowing: the minimum payment is designed to be the smallest amount that keeps an account in good standing, not the smartest amount to actually pay.

This connects to two other terms from today's vocabulary. The credit limit matters here too — a $300 balance on a $1,000 limit is using 30% of what's available, while the same $300 balance on a $350 limit is using nearly all of it. Staying well under the limit, and paying more than the minimum whenever possible, both matter — and both come back around in Day 4, where "amounts owed" turns out to be one of the biggest factors in a credit score. And the grace period is the easiest way to avoid this trap entirely: pay the full statement balance before it ends, and none of this interest math ever applies in the first place.

The APR itself isn't fixed across every card, either — it depends heavily on the type of credit, which is exactly what Day 3 dives into. Just to preview how much that matters: run that same $300 balance and $25 payment at 28% APR instead of 20% (28% ÷ 12 ≈ 2.33% per month), and month one's interest jumps to $300 × 2.33% ≈ $7.00 — about $2 more than at 20% APR, in the very first month alone. That gap compounds every single month the balance is carried, so a card with a higher APR doesn't just cost "a little more" — it can meaningfully stretch out how long a balance takes to disappear.

It's also worth knowing that not every credit card fee is interest. Some cards charge an annual fee just for keeping the account open, and a cash advance — using a credit card to withdraw cash instead of making a purchase — usually comes with its own separate, often higher fee and interest rate that starts immediately, with no grace period at all. None of these extra charges show up in the balance-and-APR math above, but they're real costs that add on top of it, which is exactly why reading a card's terms before using it matters as much as watching the APR.

Day 3 · Types of Credit: Comparing Interest Rates

NJSLS 9.1.8.CDM.2
Today's goalI can compare and calculate the interest rates associated with different types of credit.

Not all borrowed money costs the same. Typical rate ranges look roughly like this: credit cards around 18–25% APR, store or retail cards often higher — roughly 25–30% APR, personal loans from a bank or credit union often lower — roughly 8–15% APR, student loans (just a preview here — Grade 8 covers these in depth), and payday loans at extremely high rates, often 300%+ APR when calculated annually. That last one is a serious warning example, not a typical or recommended borrowing option.

Rates differ because lenders charge more when lending is riskier for them or less regulated, and less when a loan is secured, government-backed, or the borrower already has strong credit — which is exactly what Day 4's credit score lesson connects to.

Numbers make this real. Imagine borrowing $500 for one year, with four different ways to get it. To find what each option actually costs, use one simple formula for a full year of interest: Interest = Amount borrowed × Rate. Here's what that looks like for all four types of credit:

Credit card, 20% APR: Interest = $500 × 0.20 = $100. Total repaid: $500 + $100 = $600.

Store/retail card, 28% APR: Interest = $500 × 0.28 = $140. Total repaid: $500 + $140 = $640.

Personal loan, 10% APR: Interest = $500 × 0.10 = $50. Total repaid: $500 + $50 = $550.

Payday loan, 300% APR: Interest = $500 × 3.00 = $1,500. Total repaid: $500 + $1,500 = $2,000.

Look at that spread: borrowing the exact same $500 for the exact same year costs anywhere from $50 to $1,500 in interest alone, depending only on which type of credit is used. The personal loan is cheapest here because banks and credit unions are more tightly regulated and typically check a borrower's ability to repay before lending. The payday loan is catastrophically expensive because it's a short-term, high-risk loan built around borrowers who need cash fast and have few other options — lenders charge extremely high rates to make up for how risky that lending is for them, and because payday loans are less regulated than bank loans in many places.

Ranking these four from safest to riskiest for a borrower looks like: personal loan, then credit card, then store card, then payday loan — cheapest and most regulated to most expensive and least regulated. None of this means every use of credit is dangerous. A responsibly used credit card, paid in full, costs nothing extra at all. It means the type of credit matters just as much as whether credit is used at all — comparing rates before borrowing is a real, usable skill, not just a warning.

Store cards are a good case study for why the fine print matters. They often get pushed at the checkout counter with a tempting offer — "save 20% on today's purchase if you sign up right now" — and that discount is real. But that same card usually carries one of the higher APRs on this list, so if the balance isn't paid off quickly, the interest charged later can easily erase the discount and then some. A payday loan is an even sharper warning: it's marketed as "fast cash, no credit check," which sounds convenient when money is tight, but the 300%+ APR exists because the lender is taking on very little risk-screening while charging an enormous amount to make up for it. That combination — little screening, sky-high cost — is exactly what regulators in many states have stepped in to limit.

The bigger pattern to remember: a lower rate almost always comes with more steps to qualify (a credit check, proof of income, a relationship with a bank), while a higher rate almost always means faster, easier access to cash. Recognizing that trade-off — and doing the multiplication before borrowing, the way the $500 example above did — is the actual skill this lesson is teaching.

There's one more wrinkle worth knowing: not every type of credit charges interest the exact same way. A credit card charges interest only on whatever balance is left unpaid after the grace period, the way Day 2 walked through in detail. A personal loan usually works differently — it's often an installment loan, meaning the amount borrowed, the interest rate, and the length of time to repay it are all fixed from day one, and the borrower makes the same set payment every month until it's paid off completely. That predictability is actually part of why personal loans tend to carry lower rates: the lender knows exactly when they'll be repaid, which is less risky for them than an open-ended credit card balance that could be carried indefinitely.

It also helps to notice that "rate" isn't the only number that matters when comparing credit. A loan with a slightly lower APR but a much longer repayment period can sometimes end up costing more in total interest than a shorter loan at a slightly higher rate — because interest has more months to add up. That's not a reason to ignore APR; it's a reason to look at the total cost, not just the percentage, whenever it's possible to compare both. The $500-for-one-year examples above kept the timeframe the same on purpose, specifically so the rate alone could be compared fairly — real-world comparisons need to account for time too.

Try sizing this up for someone else. Malik needs $500 for a school trip deposit and has two options: his family's credit union offers a personal loan at 9% APR, repaid over one year, or a payday lender near his house offers cash today with a fee equivalent to roughly 350% APR, due back in two weeks. Running the numbers the way this lesson taught: the personal loan would cost about $500 × 0.09 = $45 in interest over the full year. The payday loan's cost depends on how quickly it's repaid, but its sky-high annualized rate exists specifically because it's designed around very short repayment windows — miss that window, and the cost can spiral fast through additional fees or a rolled-over loan. Malik's best move is obvious once the numbers are actually written out side by side, which is exactly the habit this lesson is building: never take a lender's word for what something "basically costs" — do the multiplication first.

Day 4 · Your Credit Score: What It Is & Why It Matters

NJSLS 9.1.8.CP.3
Today's goalI can explain the purpose of a credit score and credit record, and describe the factors and real-world impact of credit scores.

A credit score is a three-digit number, typically 300–850, that summarizes how reliably a person has borrowed and repaid money. A credit record (or report) is the detailed history behind that number — every account, payment, and balance that feeds into the score.

Five main factors shape it, and each one carries a different weight. Payment history (roughly 35% of the score, the single biggest factor) is simply whether bills have been paid on time. Amounts owed (roughly 30%) measures how much of your available credit you're actually using — using a small share of your limit is better than running it close to maxed out. Length of credit history (roughly 15%) reflects how long you've had accounts open — a longer, well-managed history helps. New credit (roughly 10%) tracks how often you apply for new credit — applying for several accounts in a short window can hurt your score. Credit mix (roughly 10%) rewards responsibly managing different types of credit, like a credit card and a loan, rather than just one.

Real examples make these five factors much less abstract. Someone who pays every bill on time for two years is building strong payment history. Someone who misses a credit card payment by 45 days is hurting that same factor. Someone who maxes out a credit card right up to its limit is hurting amounts owed — while someone who keeps their balance under 10% of the limit is helping it. Someone who opens five new credit accounts in a single month is hurting new credit, while someone who's kept the same account open responsibly for ten years is helping length of credit history. And someone with both a credit card and a personal loan, both paid responsibly, is helping their credit mix. None of these are dramatic events — they're the ordinary, everyday habits from Days 1 through 3 of this unit, adding up over time.

A credit score has real, everyday impact. It affects whether someone is approved for a loan or credit card at all, and what interest rate they're offered if approved — better scores usually mean lower rates, connecting straight back to Day 3's rate comparisons. It can even affect renting an apartment or, in some cases, job applications. Scores are generally grouped into tiers: roughly 300–579 is poor (loan applications are often denied, and approved loans usually come with the highest rates); 580–669 is fair (some approval is possible, usually with higher-than-average rates); 670–739 is good (most applications are approved with competitive rates); and 740–850 is excellent (the best approval odds and the lowest available rates).

Here's a concrete way to see why that tier gap matters: two people each want to borrow the same amount for the same purchase. One has an excellent score and gets offered a low rate; the other has a poor score and gets offered a much higher rate — meaning the exact same purchase, financed the exact same way, ends up costing the second person noticeably more in interest, purely because of their credit history. (That's the same math from Day 3 — a lower rate always means a lower total cost for the same amount borrowed.) A credit score isn't an abstract, unrelated number — it's built directly out of the everyday habits from Days 1–3: paying on time, keeping balances low, and borrowing carefully.

A credit record is kept and updated by companies called credit bureaus, which collect information reported by banks, credit card companies, and lenders. It's worth knowing what's not part of this system, too: a credit score isn't based on age, income, savings, or where someone lives — it's based specifically on borrowing and repayment behavior. That means a person with a modest income who always pays on time and keeps balances low can have an excellent score, while a person with a much higher income who misses payments or maxes out cards can have a poor one. The score reflects reliability as a borrower, not wealth.

It's also worth being patient with this idea: a credit score isn't built overnight, and it isn't wrecked overnight by one small slip either — it's the ongoing pattern across all five factors that matters most. Someone just starting to use credit responsibly today is already building toward a stronger score months and years from now, the same way Priya's small, always-paid-in-full purchases from Day 1 were quietly doing exactly that.

It's worth being precise about who actually sees a credit report, too. Lenders check it when someone applies for a credit card or loan, and landlords or employers sometimes check a version of it with permission — but a credit report isn't public information anyone can look up freely, and by law a person is entitled to check their own report for free on a regular basis. Reviewing it regularly matters for a reason beyond curiosity: reports can occasionally contain mistakes, like a payment that was actually made on time but got reported late by accident, and catching an error early — before it drags a score down for months — is part of managing credit responsibly.

A quick worked comparison makes the score-to-rate connection concrete. Picture two people, each borrowing the same $1,000 for a year. One has a good score and gets offered 10% APR; using the same math from Day 3, that's $1,000 × 0.10 = $100 in interest for the year. The other has a poor score and gets offered 22% APR instead: $1,000 × 0.22 = $220 in interest for the exact same loan. That's a $120 difference — for identical borrowing, on an identical amount, for an identical purpose — created entirely by the gap between the two credit scores. Multiply that same kind of gap across a car loan, or years later a mortgage for a home, and the dollar difference stops being small change and starts being thousands of dollars.

None of the five factors work in isolation, either — they interact. Someone who opens a new credit card (touching new credit) right before applying for a car loan could see two effects at once: a small short-term dip from the new inquiry, but also, over time, a lower amounts owed percentage if that new card raises their total available credit while spending stays the same. This is exactly why the goal isn't to memorize one "correct" move — it's to understand the five levers well enough to make sensible decisions, the same skill Day 5's final scenario asks for.

Day 5 · Unit Review & Assessment: Credit Decision Simulation

Synthesis: CDM.1–2; CP.3
Today's goalI can compare credit and debit options, calculate the cost of borrowing, and explain how a choice could affect a credit score over time.

Five days ago you started with the basic difference between credit and debit. Since then you've calculated what a balance actually costs when only the minimum gets paid (Day 2), compared interest rates across different types of credit (Day 3), and learned what actually shapes a credit score (Day 4).

A few mix-ups worth clearing up before this unit wraps. A credit card is not free money — it's borrowed money, and it costs real interest if not paid in full. A credit limit is a maximum you're allowed to borrow, not a target or recommended amount to spend. And a credit score isn't some distant, unrelated number — it's built directly out of the everyday habits from Days 1–4: paying on time, keeping balances low, and borrowing carefully.

Let's put all four days together with one example — the same kind of thinking today's official check-in will ask for. Imagine a friend has a $200 repair bill and three ways to cover it: put it on a credit card at 22% APR and pay it off over a few months, take a payday loan that charges a $75 fee due back in two weeks, or borrow it interest-free from a family member. First, size up that payday loan fee the way Day 3 taught: $75 ÷ $200 = 0.375, or 37.5% — and that's the cost for just two weeks, not a whole year, which makes it an extraordinarily expensive way to borrow. Compare that to the credit card: at 22% APR, paid down responsibly over a few months, the interest cost would be a small fraction of $200 — nowhere close to $75. The interest-free family loan costs nothing in interest at all, though it depends on having a family member able and willing to help.

Which option makes the most sense depends on the details, but the reasoning is what this whole unit was building toward: figure out whose money is actually being spent (Day 1), calculate what borrowing would actually cost in real numbers (Days 2 and 3), and think ahead to how the choice — paid responsibly or not — would affect a credit score down the road (Day 4). That's the same four-step thinking behind every credit decision, whether it's $200 today or a much bigger purchase years from now.

Quick vocabulary recap before the checkpoint: credit is borrowing money now with a promise to repay later, usually with interest if not paid in full; debit is spending money already sitting in a bank account, right now; APR is the yearly cost of borrowing; minimum payment is the smallest amount required each month to avoid a late fee, not a smart target to aim for; credit limit is the maximum allowed to borrow, not a spending goal; and a credit score is the three-digit summary, typically 300–850, of how reliably someone has borrowed and repaid money over time, built from payment history, amounts owed, length of credit history, new credit, and credit mix.

This unit deliberately stayed introductory — the vocabulary and the mental model. Grade 8 comes back to these exact same ideas with more depth: how to actually build and protect a strong credit profile, and how to manage credit, loans, and debt as the amounts and decisions get bigger. Today's checkpoint and reflection pull this whole unit together into one scenario of your own, just like the official check-in will.

One more way to pull the whole unit together: notice how each day actually depended on the one before it. Day 1's core idea — whose money is actually being spent — is what makes Day 2's interest math matter in the first place, since interest only exists because credit involves borrowed money. Day 2's math is what makes Day 3's rate comparisons meaningful, since knowing how interest builds up is exactly what makes a 9% loan clearly cheaper than a 300% payday loan for the identical amount. And Days 1 through 3, taken together, are what actually build the habits — paying on time, keeping balances low, borrowing carefully — that Day 4 showed get reported into a credit score. None of these four days is really separate; they're one connected skill viewed from four angles.

Try the full four-step process on one more scenario before the checkpoint. Suppose a classmate is offered a "buy now, pay later" plan for a $120 pair of shoes: no interest if paid off in four installments over six weeks, but a $10 fee automatically added if any installment is late. Step one (Day 1): this is still borrowed money, even though it's not a traditional credit card — the shoes are being paid for before the money is fully available. Step two (Days 2–3): calculate the real cost. If every installment is paid on time, the total cost stays $120 — genuinely $0 in extra cost, which is unusual and worth recognizing when it's true. But miss one payment, and the $10 fee is $10 ÷ $120 ≈ 8.3% added onto just one portion of the purchase — small compared to a payday loan, but not nothing, and avoidable entirely. Step three (Day 4): some buy-now-pay-later plans do report to credit bureaus and some don't, so it's worth checking — because if it's reported, missed payments could affect a score exactly like a missed credit card payment would. Running through those steps takes under a minute and turns a vague "should I do this?" into an actual, informed decision — which is the whole point of this unit.
