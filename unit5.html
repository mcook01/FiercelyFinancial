Day 1 · How Advertising Works: Comparing Ad Messages

NJSLS 9.1.8.FP.6
Today's goalI can compare and contrast advertising messages to identify the technique(s) they use and understand what the ad is trying to accomplish.

Every ad you scroll past, every commercial you sit through, and every sponsored post in your feed has a job to do: persuade you to buy something, support something, or believe something. But not every ad has the same job. Some ads push a direct, immediate sale — a banner screaming "50% off today only" wants you to click and buy right now. Others are playing a longer game: a soft-drink commercial with no price, no discount, and no "buy now" button at all is just trying to keep the brand's name and feeling in your head, so that next time you're standing in front of a cooler, you reach for that can without even thinking about it. Learning to ask "what is this ad actually trying to get me to do — buy now, or just like the brand?" is the first step to reading an ad clearly instead of just absorbing it.

A quick way to tell which goal you're looking at: does the ad tell you exactly what to do right now — a price, a deadline, a "buy" or "shop" button? That's a direct-sale ad. Does it just make you feel good about the brand without asking for anything specific today? That's brand-building. Most ads you'll analyze this week lean clearly one way or the other, and naming which one you're looking at helps you understand what the ad actually wants from you before you even get to its technique.

Ads rely on a handful of repeatable techniques, and once you know them, you'll start spotting them everywhere. Emotional appeal makes you feel something — happiness, nostalgia, fear of missing out — instead of giving you facts. Picture a commercial for soup that shows a cozy kitchen, a warm bowl, and a voiceover saying "nothing says home like this simmering on the stove" — it never mentions ingredients, price, or nutrition, because it doesn't need to; it's selling a feeling. Testimonial/endorsement has a person vouch for the product so you'll trust it too — like a pro skateboarder saying "these are the only shoes I trust on the ramp," even though plenty of other shoes would work just as well. Bandwagon tells you "everyone else already has one," the way a phone ad might say "join the millions who've already switched — don't get left behind!" It's not evidence the product is good; it's peer pressure dressed up as marketing.

Scarcity/urgency pressures a fast decision with phrases like "limited time" or "only 3 left in stock," so you buy before you've had a chance to think it through — a doorbuster banner that reads "sale ends TONIGHT — only 5 left!" is designed to make your decision faster than your judgment. Humor makes an ad memorable through a joke or a funny character rather than any real information about the product — think of a cereal mascot who trips over his own shoelaces in every single commercial; it's entertaining, but it tells you nothing about how the cereal tastes. Comparison positions a product directly against a competitor, like a phone company claiming its battery lasts "2 hours longer than the other guys" — which can be honest and useful information, or can quietly leave out the details that would make the comparison less flattering.

Here's a worked example of reading two ads side by side, the same way you'll be asked to on today's self-check. Ad A, for a sneaker brand, says: "Worn by 3 Olympic gold medalists. Feel like a champion the moment you lace up. Limited pairs available this week — order now before they're gone!" Ad B, for a competing sneaker brand, says: "Breathable mesh upper, extra arch support, and a 90-day comfort guarantee. Rated 4.6 out of 5 stars by over 12,000 verified buyers." Reading Ad A closely, you can spot testimonial/endorsement ("worn by 3 Olympic gold medalists"), emotional appeal ("feel like a champion"), and scarcity/urgency ("limited pairs…before they're gone") all stacked together — and notice that it never once describes the actual shoe. Ad B barely uses persuasion technique at all; it just states specific, checkable facts: the material, a support feature, a guarantee length, and a large, verifiable rating. Neither approach is automatically dishonest, but Ad B gives you far more real information to evaluate before you buy.

Every ad is also built with a specific target audience in mind — the particular group of people the ad is designed to reach and persuade. A cleat commercial airing during a soccer match, full of fast cuts and stadium noise, is aimed at a very different audience than a quiet, slow-paced ad for a retirement savings plan airing during a nightly news broadcast. Advertisers choose where an ad appears, what music plays, which techniques it leans on, and even what app or platform it shows up in based on who they think is watching. Once you notice who an ad seems to be talking to, it gets easier to notice how it's talking to them — a lot of ads aimed at people your age lean hard on bandwagon and humor, because those techniques tend to land well with a younger audience, while ads aimed at adults making a big purchase, like a car or an insurance plan, more often lean on comparison and specific numbers.

It also helps to name the exact moment an ad wants you to act — advertisers call this the call to action: the specific instruction an ad gives you, like "Shop now," "Sign up today," "Swipe up," or "Call this number." A call to action is a useful clue on its own. If an ad's call to action is something soft like "Learn more" or has no call to action at all, that's a sign it's leaning toward brand-building. If the call to action is sharp and immediate — "Buy now before midnight" — that's a strong sign it's a direct-sale ad, and probably worth a slower, more careful look before you follow it.

Put all three ideas together — goal, technique, and audience — and you have a simple three-question routine you can run on any ad you see this week: What is this ad's goal (a direct sale, or brand-building)? What technique or techniques is it using to get there? And who does it seem to be aimed at, based on where you saw it and how it's written? Try running that routine on the very next ad you notice, whether it's a video, a poster, or a sponsored post — the more ads you practice on, the faster naming these pieces becomes.

None of the six techniques above are automatically dishonest — that's an important distinction for tomorrow. A funny commercial, a genuine celebrity endorsement, or an honest comparison is just persuasion doing its normal job, and recognizing the technique doesn't mean the ad is lying to you. What separates ordinary persuasion like this from something sneakier — a claim that's actually false, or a detail that's deliberately hidden — is exactly what Day 2 digs into.

Day 2 · Deceptive Advertising: Spotting the Techniques

NJSLS 9.1.8.FP.7
Today's goalI can identify the techniques and effects of deceptive advertising, and explain what separates a persuasive technique from a deceptive claim.

Deceptive advertising is advertising that uses misleading claims or hidden information to trick a buyer, rather than honestly persuading them. That's an important line to draw: a persuasive technique alone — like yesterday's emotional appeal or humor — isn't automatically deceptive. Deception specifically requires something to be false, exaggerated beyond what's true, or hidden from you on purpose. If you can't point to a specific claim that's untrue or a detail that's been concealed, it's probably just ordinary persuasion, not deception.

Six techniques show up again and again, and each one is easier to spot once you've seen a concrete example of it in action.

Bait-and-switch advertises one product at a great price to get you in the door, then claims it's unavailable and pushes a pricier item instead. Picture a store advertising a TV for $199; you show up, and a salesperson tells you it "just sold out" and steers you toward a $599 model instead. The $199 TV may never have really been in stock at all — its whole purpose was to get you into the store.

Exaggerated or unsubstantiated claims make a big promise — "clinically proven," "doctors agree," "erases wrinkles overnight" — with no real evidence behind it: no named study, no source, no way to check it. Compare that to a claim like "9 out of 10 dentists recommend this toothpaste, according to a 2024 survey of 500 dentists" — that's specific, sourced, and checkable, which is what turns a claim from a guess you're asked to trust into a fact you could actually verify.

Hidden fees or fine print advertise a low headline price, then tack on extra costs only revealed at checkout. Here's a worked example: an online ad lists a jacket at "$19.99!" You click buy, and at checkout you discover $15 shipping, $10 handling, and a $5 processing fee — none of it mentioned anywhere in the ad. Add it up: $19.99 + $15 + $10 + $5 = $49.99. The real price is more than double the advertised one, and you only find out after you've already decided to buy.

Fake urgency claims a deal is "limited time" or "limited edition" when it isn't actually limited at all. A hoodie advertised with a banner reading "Limited Edition — Buy Now!" that has actually been in stock, completely unchanged, for over a year is a textbook example — the word "limited" is doing all the persuading, and none of it is true.

Misleading before/after uses staged conditions, filters, or unrealistic demonstrations so a product looks far better than it performs in real life. A blender commercial might show a whole watermelon turning into smooth juice in 3 seconds flat — but the actual product on store shelves takes several minutes, can't handle a rind, and would burn out trying what the commercial showed.

And undisclosed sponsorship is an ad that doesn't clearly say it's an ad — like a video that looks like a genuine, unpaid recommendation from someone you follow, when in reality the brand paid them to say it and never required them to disclose that. Because it doesn't look like an ad, you evaluate it the way you'd evaluate a friend's honest opinion, which is exactly the trust the sponsor is quietly borrowing.

A simple three-question check works for spotting deception in almost any ad. First: is there a specific, checkable claim, or just a vague promise dressed up in confident-sounding words? Second: is any part of the real cost, terms, or availability hidden until later, revealed only in fine print or at checkout? Third: does the "limited" or "urgent" framing match reality, or would the exact same deal probably still be there tomorrow, next week, or next month? If the answer to any of those three raises a red flag, that's your signal to slow down before you buy. Deceptive advertising works best on a rushed decision made in the moment — pausing just long enough to ask these three questions is often enough to break its effect completely, even before you can prove anything is wrong.

Let's run that three-question check on a full example, step by step, the way you'll be asked to on today's self-check. Here's the ad: "NEW mystery box — guaranteed $100+ value inside for just $19.99! Free shipping included. Order today!" When you click "order today," a new screen appears with a box labeled "Standard Shipping — $12.99" already checked, and the only way to get the advertised "free shipping" is a separate $9.99 "membership" you'd have to notice and decline. Question one: is there a checkable claim? "Guaranteed $100+ value" sounds specific, but nobody has said what's actually inside, so there's nothing to check — that's an exaggerated, unsubstantiated claim on its own. Question two: is any cost hidden? Yes — the "free" shipping turns out to require paying for something else, which is exactly the hidden-fee pattern from a few paragraphs above, just dressed up in a new shape. Question three: is anything falsely urgent? "Order today" implies the deal disappears tomorrow, but a mystery box with a made-up "guaranteed value" almost certainly doesn't actually run out on any particular day. All three checks raise a flag here, which is a strong sign this ad is worth skipping entirely, not just reading more carefully.

It's worth noticing that a single ad rarely uses just one deceptive technique in isolation — the mystery-box example above stacked an unsubstantiated claim, a hidden fee, and fake urgency all at once, and that stacking is common. Techniques reinforce each other: fake urgency makes you less likely to slow down and notice the hidden fee, and an unsubstantiated claim gives urgency something exciting to attach itself to. The more techniques you can name working together in a single ad, the more confident you can be that something about it deserves a second, slower look before you trust it or pay for anything.

These techniques matter beyond just being annoying — they cost people money, waste their time, and erode trust in advertising generally. Recognizing them is the actual defense: once you can name the technique at work, and point to the exact claim that's false or the exact detail that's hidden, it's a lot harder for it to work on you.

Day 3 · Comparing Prices When Ads Are Trying to Trick You

NJSLS 9.1.8.CP.1, EG.9
Today's goalI can apply comparison-shopping skills to verify an advertised discount, and identify common types of advertising-related consumer fraud.

Back in Unit 3 you learned to comparison shop — checking price, quality, and total value across different sellers before buying. Today that skill gets a trickier job, because sometimes the advertised discount itself needs to be verified in two different ways: is the percentage math actually correct, and was the "original" price ever a real, typical price to begin with?

Start with the math. Stores use a specific formula to calculate a real percent off: (Original Price − Sale Price) ÷ Original Price × 100. Here's a full worked example. A skincare serum is advertised with "Was $80, now only $60 — that's 50% OFF!" Let's check that claim: $80 − $60 = $20. Then $20 ÷ $80 = 0.25. Then 0.25 × 100 = 25%. The real discount is 25% off, not 50%. The math in the ad simply doesn't add up — and once you can run this calculation yourself in under a minute, you never have to take a store's claimed percentage on faith again.

Now the second check: even when the percentage math is correct, the "original" price itself can be fake. Picture a backpack advertised as "Was $60, now $54 — Huge Savings!" Running the formula: ($60 − $54) ÷ $60 × 100 = 10% off. Ten percent isn't nothing, but it's a long way from "huge savings" — a vague phrase is often standing in for a small, unimpressive number. Worse, a seller can simply invent an inflated "original" price that was never real to begin with, just to make any discount off of it look bigger. That's why the second half of verifying a deal is comparing the sale price — not the claimed discount — to what similar items typically cost elsewhere. If headphones are "on sale" for $25 and every other store sells the same or similar headphones for $22–$28 anyway, the "sale" isn't really saving you anything; the store just picked a bigger number to write down as the "original" price. A deal is only genuine when both checks pass: the percentage math is accurate, and the sale price is actually lower than what you'd pay elsewhere for a comparable item.

Finding a "typical market price" doesn't require anything fancy: search the exact item name (or a close match) on two or three different retailer websites, note the range of prices you see, and use that range — not the single "original" price the ad gave you — as your reality check. If three different stores all sell something similar for around $25, and an ad's "sale" price is $25, the original "$50" price tag was probably inflated. If the ad's price is genuinely below that range, the deal may be real — though an unusually low price from an unfamiliar seller is also exactly the pattern fake online stores and counterfeit sellers use, which is why price alone is never the only thing worth checking.

That same skepticism is the first line of defense against a narrow but important slice of consumer fraud tied directly to advertising. A fake online store is a slick-looking website that takes your payment for items that are low quality, wrong, or never arrive at all — then the site disappears. Counterfeit goods are fake "brand-name" items sold as if they were genuine, often through social media or pop-up ads at prices dramatically lower than any real retailer would ever charge — think a "designer" bag at 90% off from a website you've never heard of, with no listed return policy. A "free trial" trap requires your card number up front and quietly starts charging it automatically once the trial period ends, with the auto-renewal terms often buried in a long paragraph of fine print you'd have to go looking for. And phishing disguised as a promotion uses a fake "you've won!" message or an urgent prize pop-up — like "You're our 1,000,000th visitor — claim your prize!" — to get you to type in personal or payment information, for a prize that was never real to begin with.

The habit that protects you against all four fraud types and both discount tricks is the same: before trusting an advertised price or claim, slow down and check it — run the percentage math yourself, compare the price to what similar items cost elsewhere, and ask whether you actually recognize and trust the seller. If a deal looks dramatically better than everywhere else, or a message creates pressure to act immediately, that combination is the red flag worth pausing on — not automatic proof of a scam, but always worth a second look before you buy or enter any information. (Full detail on reporting fraud and consumer protection law is coming in Unit 8 — today is just about recognizing the advertising-related warning signs.)

Let's put the whole process together on one more full example, start to finish. An ad reads: "Was $150, NOW $70 — 60% OFF! Limited stock, free returns." First, the math: ($150 − $70) ÷ $150 × 100 = $80 ÷ $150 = 0.533…, so 0.533 × 100 ≈ 53%. That's actually close to the advertised 60% — not exact, but not a wild exaggeration either, so the percentage check comes back mostly fine. Second, the market check: you search the item on three other retailer sites and find it typically sells for $65–$75. Since $70 falls right in that normal range, the "sale" price is a genuinely typical price, not a special bargain — the item was probably never really $150 to begin with, and the "original" price was likely inflated just to make the math look more dramatic. Notice that even though the percentage was roughly accurate, the deal still isn't the steal it's advertised to be, because the sale price matches what you'd pay anywhere else anyway. Both checks matter, and they can point in different directions on the very same ad.

One more habit worth building alongside the math: read who's selling the item, not just what it costs. A familiar, established retailer offering $70 on an item that typically runs $65–$75 is just an ordinary, reasonable price — nothing suspicious there. But that exact same price, from a brand-new website you've never heard of, with no listed return address and only one payment option, deserves a second look before you buy, even though the number itself checked out. The math tells you whether a discount is real; the seller tells you whether the purchase itself is safe. A smart shopper checks both, every time, not just whichever one is easier to look up.

Day 4 · Unit Review & Assessment: Ad Detective

Synthesis: FP.6–7; CP.1; EG.9
Today's goalI can analyze an advertisement for technique, deception, and fraud risk, and verify whether its price claim looks genuine.

Three days ago you started with the basic techniques every ad uses (Day 1), then learned what separates ordinary persuasion from outright deception (Day 2), and applied comparison shopping to verify advertised discounts and spot fraud red flags (Day 3). Today pulls all three together into one skill: reading a single ad closely enough to catch everything going on inside it — exactly what "Ad Detective" work means, and exactly what today's official check-in will ask you to do.

Let's walk through a full example the way a real Ad Detective would. Here's the ad under investigation: "FLASH SALE! Everyone's grabbing the new GlowSkin Serum before it's gone — clinically proven to erase wrinkles in just ONE night! Was $80, now only $60 — that's 50% OFF! As seen recommended by top influencers. Only a few bottles left — act now!"

Step 1 — find the techniques. Read it line by line. "Everyone's grabbing" is bandwagon. "Clinically proven to erase wrinkles in just ONE night," with no study or source named, is an unsubstantiated claim — a specific, science-sounding word like "clinically" is doing a lot of work with nothing behind it. "Only a few bottles left — act now" is scarcity/urgency. And "recommended by top influencers" is a vague, uncredited endorsement — which influencers, and were they paid to say it? The ad doesn't say.

Step 2 — verify the math. The ad claims 50% off from $80 to $60. Run the formula from Day 3: ($80 − $60) ÷ $80 × 100. That's $20 ÷ $80 = 0.25, then 0.25 × 100 = 25%. The real discount is 25% off, not 50%. This alone tells you the ad is willing to state a number that's flatly untrue, not just exaggerate a feeling.

Step 3 — deliver a verdict. Putting it together: this ad stacks bandwagon, an unsubstantiated medical-sounding claim, manufactured urgency, and a vague endorsement — and its one specific, checkable number turns out to be wrong by double. A reasonable verdict: this ad is not trustworthy, and the inaccurate discount math is the strongest single piece of evidence, because everything else is a feeling or a vague claim, while the math is something you proved false yourself.

Notice that all three steps above reuse skills from earlier days: Step 1 is the technique-spotting from Day 1 and Day 2, and Step 2 is the percent-off formula from Day 3. That's the whole point of an Ad Detective — it isn't a new skill, it's the same moves, run one after another, on a single ad. Don't forget Day 3's fraud check either: even an ad with an accurate discount and no obviously deceptive technique can still be a fraud risk if the seller is unfamiliar, the price is dramatically below anywhere else, or the payment method offers no buyer protection. A complete Ad Detective verdict weighs technique, honesty, math, and seller trustworthiness together — not just one of the four. When your official check-in gives you a new ad to investigate, run these same steps: read it line by line and name every technique you spot, verify any specific number using (Original − Sale) ÷ Original × 100, and write a verdict that points to specific evidence — not just a feeling that the ad seems "sketchy" or "fine."

A few mix-ups worth clearing up before this unit wraps. First: not every persuasive technique is deceptive — emotional appeal, humor, and honest comparison are normal advertising, and deception specifically requires a claim that's actually false, exaggerated past what's true, or deliberately hidden. Second: not every online sale is a scam — most aren't, and treating all advertising as fraudulent is just as unhelpful as trusting all of it blindly; the goal is checking, not assuming either way. Third: a claimed "discount" is only meaningful once you've both run the percentage math yourself and checked the price against what similar items typically cost elsewhere — a technically accurate percentage can still be calculated off of a fake "original" price.

It's worth practicing this on one more ad before your official check-in, this time with a twist: an ad that looks mostly clean at first glance. "NatureBrew Iced Tea — made with real ingredients you can pronounce. Now available at a store near you." Run the routine: any techniques? Not much — "real ingredients you can pronounce" leans slightly toward comparison against artificial-sounding competitors, but it's mild and there's no call to action pushing urgency. Any false or hidden claim? None stated, and nothing to verify, because the ad makes no specific numeric or scientific claim at all. Any price or math to check? There isn't one — no price is mentioned anywhere. Any fraud risk? Almost none, since it names a specific, findable retail presence ("a store near you") rather than an unfamiliar website. The verdict here looks very different from the GlowSkin serum example: this ad is mostly just ordinary, low-pressure brand-building, with nothing to substantiate and nothing to be wary of. Not every ad you investigate will turn out deceptive — part of being a good Ad Detective is correctly clearing an ad, too, not just catching problems that aren't really there.

A few mix-ups worth clearing up before this unit wraps. First: not every persuasive technique is deceptive — emotional appeal, humor, and honest comparison are normal advertising, and deception specifically requires a claim that's actually false, exaggerated past what's true, or deliberately hidden. Second: not every online sale is a scam — most aren't, and treating all advertising as fraudulent is just as unhelpful as trusting all of it blindly; the goal is checking, not assuming either way. Third: a claimed "discount" is only meaningful once you've both run the percentage math yourself and checked the price against what similar items typically cost elsewhere — a technically accurate percentage can still be calculated off of a fake "original" price.

This unit doesn't repeat directly in Grade 8, so today's checkpoint and reflection are your last formal practice with this skill in this course — worth taking seriously, since you'll be applying it on your own from here forward, every time you see an ad, a "flash sale," or a message urging you to act right now.

An unfamiliar store, an unbelievably low price, and an unusual payment method with no buyer protection together are a classic fake-online-store pattern.

4. An ad claims "Was $200, NOW $90 — 70% OFF!" Using the discount formula, what is the actual percent off, and what does that tell you?

($200 − $90) ÷ $200 × 100 = $110 ÷ $200 × 100 = 55%, not 70% — the ad is overstating the real discount, just like the GlowSkin serum example.
