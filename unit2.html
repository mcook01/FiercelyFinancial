Day 1 · Where Does Money Come From?

NJSLS 9.2.8.CAP.3
Today's goalI can identify different types of income and explain how career choice, education, economic conditions, and personal behavior can affect how much a person earns.

Unit 1 was about the money decisions you make. This unit starts one step earlier: where does the money come from in the first place? Before you can budget, save, or set a goal, you need income — money coming in, from somewhere.

There are four main types of income, and it helps to picture a real person earning each one. Wages are paid by the hour: Maria works 15 hours a week at a grocery store for $12 an hour, so her pay changes with her schedule — more hours worked means more pay, fewer hours means less. Salary is a fixed amount paid on a regular schedule no matter how many hours the work actually takes: a graphic designer paid $52,000 a year gets the exact same amount every two weeks whether that stretch was slow or slammed. Tips and commission are pay tied to service or sales: a server might earn $8 an hour plus whatever tips customers leave, while a car salesperson earns a percentage of the price of every car they sell — do more business, earn more money. And self-employment or gig work means you're your own boss: think of someone mowing five neighbors' lawns for $25 each, or selling handmade jewelry online and keeping the profit — you set the terms, but there's no guaranteed paycheck if business is slow. (There's a fifth type, investment income — money your money itself earns, like interest — but that's a Grade 8 topic. For now, just know it exists.)

Wages and salary might sound similar, but the trade-off between them matters in real life. Maria's wage job means if she picks up an extra 5-hour shift, she earns extra money — 5 hours × $12 = $60 more that week. But it also means if the store is slow and cuts her hours to 8, her paycheck shrinks right along with it. The graphic designer's salary works the opposite way: predictable pay every two weeks, but no extra pay just because a project ran late and required extra hours to finish. Neither structure is automatically "better" — a wage gives you a direct payoff for extra effort but less certainty, while a salary gives you certainty but no extra reward for extra hours. That trade-off is worth understanding now, because it's one more thing to weigh when you're comparing two real job offers someday.

Some real situations blend more than one type, and that's normal. A food-delivery driver who earns a per-delivery rate plus tips is really doing gig work and earning tips at the same time. The point of learning these four categories isn't to force every job into exactly one box — it's to recognize the pattern behind any way a person gets paid, so you can talk about income accurately instead of just calling everything "a job."

Here's the bigger idea this unit builds on: how much a person earns over time isn't random luck. It's shaped by four things working together, and each one is easier to see with a real example. Career choice matters because different careers simply pay differently for the same number of hours worked. Education and skills matter too — picture two friends who both wanted to become dental hygienists: one took biology and health-science electives in high school and finished a dental hygiene training program, while the other skipped that extra training and took a different job instead. The one with the training got hired at a higher starting wage — not luck, but the direct result of a specific skill an employer needed. Economic conditions — the job market, minimum wage, how in-demand a skill is — work on a bigger scale: when a local factory closes because of automation and cheaper overseas competition, the workers who already had extra technical certifications tend to get hired into new positions faster than workers without them, simply because employers still need those particular skills somewhere else. And personal behavior — showing up on time, being professional, doing solid work — plays out day to day: imagine two employees doing the exact same job, where one is reliable and takes on extra responsibility while the other is frequently late and does the bare minimum. After a year, it's usually the reliable one who gets offered the raise and the promotion — because of what they consistently did, not who they know.

You don't control all four factors equally — you can't single-handedly change the whole job market. But understanding all four means you can make more informed choices about your own path, starting now, instead of leaving your future income up to chance.

One more real detail before you move on: New Jersey has child labor laws that limit the hours and type of work people under 16 can legally do — for example, restrictions on how late you can work on a school night, and which jobs aren't open to you yet at your age. Workers under 16 generally need a "working paper" — a permit signed by a school official — before they can legally start a job, and there are limits on how many hours they can work per week and how late into the evening they can work once school is in session. Certain jobs, like operating heavy machinery, simply aren't legally open to a worker under 18, no matter how capable that person might be — the law treats those jobs as too risky for a younger worker, full stop, regardless of individual skill. That's worth knowing now, before you ever start job-hunting for real; it protects you, and it explains why some jobs just aren't available to you yet, not because of anything about you personally.

Day 2 · Exploring Careers

NJSLS 9.2.8.CAP.11–12
Today's goalI can assess my own strengths and interests, and use a real career-research tool to learn about a career's education, pay, and daily tasks.

Yesterday was about income in general. Today gets personal: what career might actually fit you?

Careers tend to cluster around six broad interest areas, and most people lean toward more than one. Building/Making fits someone who likes working with their hands — building, fixing, or taking things apart (think construction, mechanics, manufacturing). Helping People fits someone who cares about others' wellbeing and likes teaching or listening (healthcare, teaching, counseling). Organizing/Business suits someone who likes planning, managing details, or leading a group (management, finance, logistics). Creating/Performing is for people drawn to drawing, writing, music, or design (art, design, music, writing). Investigating/Science fits someone curious about how things work and good at solving problems (research, engineering, medicine). And Persuading/Leading suits someone who enjoys convincing others, public speaking, or taking charge (sales, law, politics, entrepreneurship). If you read all six and thought "that's basically three of me," that's completely normal — interests overlap, and that's a strength, not a problem to fix.

Your interests aren't locked in forever, either — they're worth revisiting every year or two, because what excites you at 12 can shift by 15, and that's completely expected, not a sign you did anything wrong now. It's also common for one interest area to point toward several very different careers. Someone drawn to both Investigating/Science and Helping People, for example, might end up considering nursing, biomedical engineering, or even veterinary medicine — three different careers that all combine curiosity about how living things work with a desire to help. The point of naming your top interest area isn't to lock in a single career today; it's to narrow a huge list of possible careers down to a smaller, more manageable handful actually worth researching.

Once you have a sense of your interests, the next step is real research, not guessing. CareerOneStop and My Next Move are free tools run by the U.S. Department of Labor — the same government agency that tracks real employment data nationwide. Search a career on either site and you'll find four things: its median pay (the middle salary people in that job actually earn), the education or training it typically requires, what people in that job actually do day to day, and whether the field is projected to grow, hold steady, or shrink over the next several years. That's a far more reliable picture than a guess based on TV shows or something you heard secondhand.

Here's what that research actually looks like, worked through for one real career: a registered nurse. Median pay: around $77,000 a year. Education required: an associate's or bachelor's degree in nursing, plus passing a licensing exam. Typical daily tasks: caring for patients, giving medication, and monitoring their health. Outlook: growing, meaning demand for this job is expected to increase. Notice how specific that is — not "nurses make good money," but an actual number, an actual credential, and actual daily tasks. That's the kind of detail you're looking for when you research your own career below. A few more quick examples of that same kind of detail: an automotive service technician typically needs a postsecondary certificate plus on-the-job training and earns around $47,000 a year in a stable field; a software developer typically needs a bachelor's degree (or a coding bootcamp) and often earns well over $100,000 a year in a fast-growing field; an electrician usually trains through a paid apprenticeship rather than a college degree and earns around $60,000 a year in a growing field. Four different careers, four different combinations of education and pay — none of them "better" in general, just different trade-offs.

It helps to compare two real research results side by side to see how much this varies even within one interest area. Imagine two students who both picked Investigating/Science as their top interest. One researches marine biologist and finds: median pay around $68,000, a bachelor's degree required (often a master's for many roles), daily tasks involving studying ocean life and collecting field data, and a stable outlook. The other researches software developer and finds: median pay often over $100,000, a bachelor's degree or coding bootcamp, daily tasks involving writing and testing code, and a fast-growing outlook. Same interest area, two completely different careers, two completely different sets of facts — and neither student could have known any of that without actually looking it up. That's exactly why research beats a guess: two people who share an interest can still land on very different real-world trade-offs.

That "outlook" detail — growing, stable, or declining — is worth pausing on, because it connects straight back to Day 1's idea about economic conditions shaping income. A field can be declining because a task is being automated or handled more cheaply elsewhere (some retail and manufacturing roles look like this), while a field can be growing because the population and demand for it keep increasing (healthcare and technology often look like this). Neither pattern is a life sentence — plenty of people build great careers in fields that aren't "growing" — but it's exactly the kind of real information that guessing could never give you, and exactly why research beats a hunch.

Use the tool below to research one career connected to your top interest area. This isn't a commitment — it's information-gathering. You're allowed to research a career today and feel differently about it next year; that's exactly what this process is for.

Day 3 · Pathways to Your Career

NJSLS 9.2.8.CAP.1–2
Today's goalI can identify different education and training pathways and connect one to a career I'm interested in.

You researched a career on Day 2. Today's question: how do you actually get there?

There's no single "right" path — most careers can be reached more than one way, and the path you pick depends on the specific career and what fits you. CTE (Career and Technical Education) programs are courses or whole academies, often available in high school, that teach job-specific skills directly in a field like health sciences, engineering, culinary arts, cosmetology, or automotive work — sometimes leading straight to an industry certification. Apprenticeships pair paid on-the-job training with related classroom instruction — you earn a paycheck while you learn the trade, which is common in fields like electrician, plumber, and HVAC technician work. Dual enrollment lets you take real college-level courses while still in high school, earning high school and college credit for the same class at the same time — a genuine head start. Military training programs teach technical and leadership skills in many fields, often building skills that transfer into a related civilian career afterward. Then there's the more familiar route of college — a two-year associate degree, which can lead directly to a career or transfer into a four-year program, or a four-year bachelor's degree, which many professional careers (like engineering, teaching, or nursing) require outright. And finally, direct-entry jobs hire people with no additional schooling required first and train them on the job.

Here's why "no single right path" is more than a nice phrase — look at how differently two careers can actually be reached. Someone aiming to become a surgeon needs four years of college, then medical school, then years of additional hands-on training — there's really one long route, and it doesn't skip steps. Compare that to becoming an electrician: most electricians get there through a paid apprenticeship, learning on the job and in a classroom at the same time, with no four-year degree required at all. And some careers can genuinely be reached more than one way — take someone who wants to become a veterinary technician. They could complete a two-year associate degree program in veterinary science, or they could pursue a CTE animal-science program in high school combined with on-the-job training. Both are legitimate, realistic paths to the exact same career; which one fits better depends on things like cost, how quickly someone wants to start earning, and how they learn best.

Cost is often the hidden factor behind these choices, and it's worth naming directly. A four-year public college can cost tens of thousands of dollars including room and board, though scholarships, grants, and programs that help cover community college tuition for eligible students can significantly lower that number. An apprenticeship, by contrast, typically costs the apprentice nothing — the employer pays for the training because they need the skill, and the apprentice earns a wage the entire time they're learning. Neither path is automatically the "smart money" choice; it depends on the specific career, how much cost (if any) a family is comfortable taking on, and how quickly someone wants to be earning a full paycheck.

None of these paths is "better" in general. A four-year degree isn't automatically the smartest move, and skipping college altogether isn't automatically a shortcut, either — it depends entirely on what the specific career actually requires and what setup works for you. A graphic designer, for instance, might build toward that career through a two-year design program, a four-year degree, or a self-taught portfolio plus freelance work — employers in creative fields often care more about the portfolio than about which path produced it.

When two pathways both seem realistic for the same career, it usually comes down to a few practical questions: How soon do you want (or need) to start earning money? An apprenticeship pays you while you train; a four-year degree usually costs money for years before you earn anything from that career. How much does each path cost, and is any of that cost covered — some CTE programs and apprenticeships are free or even paid, while college usually isn't. And how do you learn best — some people thrive with hands-on, on-the-job training, while others do better with a more traditional classroom setting. There's no universal right answer to any of these questions; the point is knowing they exist so you can actually weigh them, instead of assuming there's only one door into any career.

This is also where your choices in the next few years start to matter, even before you pick a career for real. High schools often let students choose electives, and some offer whole CTE academies inside the building — a health-sciences academy, an engineering academy, a culinary program. Picking one of those isn't a lifelong commitment, but it is a low-risk way to test-drive a field before investing years and money in it. Someone who spends two years in a health-sciences academy and realizes partway through that it's not for them hasn't wasted anything — they've learned something true about themselves for a fraction of the cost of switching majors partway through college, which is one of the more common (and expensive) mistakes people make when they skip this kind of early exploration.

Today, connect the pathways above to the career you researched yesterday, and think about one small, real step you could take now or in high school to move toward it — something as concrete as signing up for a related elective, joining a related club, or looking up whether a local CTE program or apprenticeship connects to your interest.

Day 4 · Paychecks & Your Digital Footprint

NJSLS 9.1.8.EG.1 & 9.2.8.CAP.4
Today's goalI can explain the difference between gross and net pay, and describe how my online activity could affect future opportunities.

If you earned $200 this week at a job, would you get to keep all $200? Most people's first guess is yes — but the honest answer is no, and today explains exactly why, with real numbers.

Gross pay is the total amount you earn before anything is taken out — just your hourly rate times your hours (or your salary amount). Net pay — also called take-home pay — is what's actually left after deductions come out. The most common deductions are federal income tax, state income tax, and Social Security and Medicare (often labeled together as FICA on a pay stub). Whatever remains after all of that is your disposable income — the money you actually get to spend or save.

Let's walk through a real, complete example so this math is never a mystery. Imagine a pay stub for an employee named Jordan, who worked 40 hours over a two-week pay period at an hourly rate of $14.00.

Step 1 — find gross pay. Multiply hours by hourly rate: 40 hours × $14.00 = $560.00 gross pay. That's the number before anything gets subtracted.

Step 2 — add up each deduction. Federal income tax on this paycheck comes to $56.00. State income tax (in New Jersey) comes to $16.80. Social Security is 6.2% of gross pay: 6.2% of $560.00 works out to $34.72. Medicare is 1.45% of gross pay: 1.45% of $560.00 works out to $8.12. Add all four together: $56.00 + $16.80 + $34.72 + $8.12 = $115.64 in total deductions.

Step 3 — subtract total deductions from gross pay. $560.00 − $115.64 = $444.36 net pay. That $444.36 is what actually lands in Jordan's bank account — a little over 20% of the gross pay never even reaches it.

Try the same three steps with a simpler example. Casey works 20 hours at $13 an hour, so gross pay is 20 × $13.00 = $260.00. If total deductions come out to about 18% of gross pay, that's 0.18 × $260.00 = $46.80 in deductions. Net pay is $260.00 − $46.80 = $213.20. Multiply to find gross pay, add up the deductions, subtract — same pattern every time, and once you've worked it with real numbers, it stops feeling abstract.

One common misconception is that only hourly or salaried pay gets taxed, while cash tips somehow don't count. They do — tip income is legally taxable too, even when a customer hands cash directly to a server, which is one more reason the deductions above apply across almost every kind of paid work, not just a traditional hourly job.

So on that $200 paycheck from the opening question, your real net pay would likely land somewhere around $160–$170, depending on where you live and how much you earn. (Real tax math has more detail than this — different tax brackets, other possible deductions — but this unit keeps it simple on purpose; Unit 7 goes deeper into how taxes actually work.)

The second half of today is a different kind of paycheck readiness: your digital footprint — everything you post, like, comment on, or get tagged in online. It doesn't disappear just because you moved on from it, and it's more public than it feels in the moment. Future employers and college admissions offices really do look — not to spy, but because a public online presence is, well, public. Picture four job applicants side by side. One has a public profile showing photos of community volunteer work and a post about finishing a coding project — that reads as responsible and engaged. Another's public profile includes posts using disrespectful language about coworkers from a past job — that's a red flag before the person even lands an interview. A third applicant has no public social media at all — every account is private — which simply tells an employer nothing either way. And a fourth posts publicly about being "so bored" at their current job, tagging the employer by name — which could easily get back to that very employer, or the next one who searches their name later. Same kind of platform, four very different impressions, and probably only one of those four people actually meant for an employer to see what they posted.

A simple habit helps here: before posting anything publicly, pause and ask three questions. Would you be comfortable with a teacher, a future employer, or a college admissions officer seeing this exact post? Could this post be shared or screenshotted by someone else, even if you delete it later? And does this post represent how you actually want to be seen, or just how you feel in this one moment? None of this means never posting anything — it means posting on purpose instead of by accident, which is a skill worth building well before your first job application actually depends on it.

This isn't about being scared of the internet — it's about knowing that what you post now becomes part of how people who've never met you might form a first impression later. Today's self-check covers both halves: the math of a paycheck, and the judgment call of an online presence.

Synthesis: CAP.1–4, 11–12; EG.1
Today's goalI can connect what I've learned about income, careers, education pathways, and paychecks into one overall picture of how earning works.

Four days ago you asked "where does money come from?" Here's the full picture you've built since: income comes in different types — wages, salary, tips/commission, and self-employment or gig work (Day 1). The career you choose to pursue should connect to your own strengths and interests, and real research (not guessing) tells you what a career actually pays, requires, and involves day to day (Day 2). There are multiple real pathways to get there — CTE, apprenticeships, dual enrollment, military training, college, or direct-entry jobs — and most careers can be reached more than one way (Day 3). And once you're actually earning, your paycheck involves gross pay, deductions, and net pay, plus a digital footprint that follows you into that working world (Day 4).

Two common mix-ups worth clearing up before you move on. First: gross pay and net pay are not the same thing — net pay is always less than or equal to gross pay, never more, because deductions only ever subtract. If someone tells you their "salary" is $50,000, that's their gross pay for the year; their actual take-home pay will be noticeably less once taxes and other deductions come out. Second: a four-year college degree is not the only path to a solid career. Apprenticeships, CTE programs, and direct-entry jobs are all legitimate routes into real, well-paying careers, depending on the field — the right pathway depends on the specific career, not on which path sounds the most impressive. A third mix-up is worth naming too: a career's median pay is a middle number, not a guarantee — half of everyone in that job earns more, and half earns less, depending on things like location, employer, years of experience, and everything Day 1 covered about career choice, education, economic conditions, and personal behavior. Two people with the exact same job title can end up on very different paychecks for real, explainable reasons — median pay is a helpful starting estimate, not a promise.

Here's how all four days connect in one example. Amir wants to become a veterinary technician. From Day 3, he knows a realistic pathway might be a two-year associate degree program in veterinary science, or a CTE animal-science program combined with on-the-job training — there's more than one legitimate way in. From Day 1, he knows his future income as a vet tech will be shaped by things like how much training he completes and how reliable he is as an employee, not just luck. And from Day 4, once he actually starts that job, he'll need to understand that his paycheck won't match his hourly rate times his hours worked exactly — taxes and other deductions will come out first, leaving him with a smaller net pay than his gross pay. Notice how answering one simple question, "how do I prepare for this career?", pulls together income types, pathways, and paycheck basics all at once — that's exactly the kind of connected thinking this unit was building toward.

Now imagine a version of Amir who skipped this process entirely — picked "vet tech" because it sounded nice, never researched the actual pay or education required, and never thought about pathways at all. He might discover months into a training program that it requires more schooling than he expected, or that the starting pay is lower than he assumed, or that it doesn't connect to any of his actual strengths after all. None of that is guaranteed to happen — but it's a lot more likely when a decision skips research entirely. That's the real value of this unit: not guaranteeing the "right" career, but replacing guesswork with information you can actually act on.

Here's one more way today's ideas connect directly to your Unit 1 SMART goal. Suppose your goal involves saving $150 for something specific. If you took on a part-time job earning $12 an hour for 10 hours a week, your gross pay would be $120 a week — but as Day 4 covered, your real net pay would be closer to $96–$102 a week, roughly 80–85% of that gross amount, once deductions come out. That means reaching your $150 goal would realistically take about a week and a half of real paychecks, not exactly one week like the gross number alone might suggest. That's the whole unit in miniature: income type, paycheck math, and your own goal, all in one calculation.

It's worth naming why this unit came before the ones on budgeting and taxes, not after. You can't realistically plan a budget (Unit 3) around income you don't understand, and you can't make sense of how taxes work (Unit 7) without already knowing the difference between gross and net pay. Everything you learned this week — income types, career fit, pathways, and paycheck basics — is the foundation the rest of this course keeps building on, so it's worth actually knowing this material solidly rather than rushing past it.
