Financial literacy is the knowledge and skills you need to make informed decisions about earning, spending, saving, and managing money — not just now, but for the rest of your life. It's not about how much money you have. A person with very little money and a person with a lot of money both need financial literacy; it's the difference between money happening to you and you making decisions about your money.

Before we get into budgets or bank accounts, this course starts somewhere most personal-finance classes skip: your money mindset — the beliefs and attitudes about money you've already picked up, often without realizing it. Maybe you've heard "money doesn't grow on trees," or watched a family member stress about bills, or noticed an influencer flaunting purchases online. All of that shapes how you think about money before you've even earned your first paycheck.

Psychologists sometimes call these absorbed beliefs "money scripts." Two common ones sound like this: "Money is stressful and I'd rather not think about it," versus "Money is a tool, and I can learn to use it well." Neither reaction makes you a good or bad person — but the second one is a skill you can build, regardless of where you're starting from. That's really the whole point of this course: not to tell you how to feel about money, but to give you tools so that, whatever your mindset, you're making choices on purpose instead of by accident.

Here's what that difference looks like in real life. Imagine two 7th graders, Jordan and Priya, each get a $40 birthday gift from a grandparent on the same afternoon. Jordan's money script says, "money is stressful, so don't overthink it — just enjoy it while it's here." Jordan spends the whole $40 that same day on snacks and a mobile-game purchase, and a week later can barely remember where it went. Priya's money script says, "money is a tool, and I get to decide how to use it." Priya takes a few minutes to think it over, puts $15 toward something she's been wanting, and drops the other $25 into a jar she's saving toward a bigger goal. Neither of them is a "better" person because of this — but notice that only one of them actually made a decision. The other one just let the money disappear on autopilot. That's the real difference a mindset makes: it's not about which purchase is "correct," it's about whether you're choosing on purpose or letting the choice happen to you.

You may have also heard the terms fixed mindset and growth mindset applied to school subjects like math — the same idea applies directly to money. A fixed money mindset sounds like, "I'm just bad with money, that's who I am," as if your money skills are permanently set in stone. A growth money mindset sounds like, "I'm not great at this yet, but I can learn." The growth version isn't naive optimism — it's just realistic. Budgeting, saving, and spending wisely are skills, the same way shooting a free throw or reading music are skills: nobody is born already good at them, and everyone improves with practice and the right information. That's the entire premise of this course, and it's true no matter which of the two money scripts above sounds more like you right now.

Here's a way to see how much a mindset shift is actually worth, in real numbers. Imagine two students who each decide to set aside $5 a week — about the cost of one snack run. A student who half-believes saving "isn't really worth it for someone my age" tends to skip the habit whenever the week gets busy or boring, realistically banking that $5 only every other week. Over a 36-week school year, that's about 18 weeks banked — $90 saved. A student who treats saving as a skill worth practicing, even when it's not exciting, sticks with it most weeks; over that same 36-week year, that's closer to $180 — exactly double, from the exact same $5 and the exact same number of weeks. Nothing about the amount changed. What changed was whether the habit survived a boring week — and that's really what a mindset controls: not how much money shows up, but whether you keep doing the small thing that adds up.

So how do you actually change a script you didn't choose in the first place? One simple three-step habit works for almost any money script: catch it, name it, choose it. Catch it means noticing the moment right before you spend or decide not to save — that's usually when the script is loudest. Name it means putting words to the thought, even just in your head: "this is the 'don't overthink it' voice again," or "this is the 'I'm just bad with money' voice." Choose it means deciding, on purpose, whether to follow that voice this time or try something different. This isn't about silencing the thought — you'll probably keep hearing it for a while. It's about making sure the thought doesn't get to make the decision by itself, every single time, without you noticing it happened.

You don't have to figure out your entire money mindset today — just start noticing it. Over the next few days, pay attention to the story running through your head right before you spend or save something: is it closer to Jordan's ("don't overthink it, just enjoy it") or Priya's ("I get to decide how to use this")? Neither story is permanent. The whole point of naming it is that once you can actually hear it in the moment, you get to choose whether to follow it or write a different one.

Over the next four days you'll look at what you value, how emotions influence spending, and how to set a real goal for your own money. Think of today as setting the foundation everything else gets built on.

Day 2 · Needs, Wants & Values

NJSLS 9.1.8.FP.2–3
Today's goalI can distinguish needs from wants and explain how my personal values influence my spending choices.

Every spending decision starts with a question, even if you don't say it out loud: is this a need or a want? A need is something required for your health, safety, or basic functioning — food, shelter, required school supplies, a winter coat when it's cold. A want makes life more fun, comfortable, or exciting, but you could live without it — the newest game, a second pair of sneakers, extra snacks.

Most real decisions aren't that clean, though. Take a smartphone: for a teen who needs it to reach a job or coordinate with family, it functions like a need. For the same phone used only for games and social media, it's closer to a want. Context — your situation, your responsibilities — changes the category.

That's where values come in: the things that matter most to you, like family, security, creativity, adventure, generosity, or belonging. Two people can have the exact same amount of money and spend it completely differently, and neither is "wrong" — they're making choices that reflect what they value. A student who values creativity might spend a gift card on art supplies; a student who values long-term security might save the same amount toward something bigger. Knowing your own values in advance makes spending decisions easier, because you're comparing a purchase to what actually matters to you — not guessing in the moment.

Let's walk through a real "depends" decision step by step, since those are the trickiest. Say your backpack's zipper finally breaks and you need a new one — that part is a clear need; you can't carry your books without a working bag. But when you go shopping, you find two options: a plain backpack for $28 that holds everything you need, and a backpack from your favorite brand for $75 with the exact same capacity. Which one is the "need"? Neither, exactly — the need is simply a working backpack, and the $28 option already covers that completely. The extra $47 is a want, and whether it's worth spending depends entirely on your values. If one of your top values is fitting in with your friend group or expressing yourself through what you own, that extra cost might genuinely be worth it to you. If your top value is security, or you're saving toward something bigger, you'd probably skip it and keep the difference. Notice what didn't change: the backpack does the exact same job either way. What changed was which value was doing the deciding — and that's a completely reasonable way to make the call, as long as you're doing it on purpose instead of out of habit.

A quick three-question test can help you sort tricky "depends" cases like this one whenever you run into them: (1) Would going without this cause a real problem for my health, safety, or ability to function — not just disappointment? (2) Does my specific situation change the answer (a phone that's required for a job versus a phone used only for games)? (3) If it's genuinely a "depends," which of my values is doing the deciding, and how much is that value actually worth to me in dollars? Running an item through these three questions turns a vague feeling — "I really want this" — into a decision you could explain out loud and still agree with a week later.

Getting this distinction right matters beyond card sorts and quizzes. Later in this course, when you build an actual budget, needs get covered first, because skipping them causes real problems — a missed bus pass can mean being late to school, and skipped groceries mean going hungry. Wants get whatever money is left over after needs are handled, and that's exactly where your values earn their keep: they help you decide which wants are genuinely worth the money to you, instead of just buying whatever happens to be in front of you.

There's a name worth knowing for this kind of trade-off: economists call it opportunity cost — the value of what you give up when you choose to spend money on one thing instead of something else. Say you have $60 total this month. If you spend $47 extra on the brand-name backpack from the earlier example, that same $47 is no longer available for, say, a $45 outing with your two closest friends next month — even though time with friends might rank higher on your values list than a backpack logo would, once you actually stop to compare them. Opportunity cost isn't a reason to never buy the want; it's a reminder that every want you buy is quietly competing against every other want you haven't bought yet, whether you think about it that way or not.

Values also don't stay fixed, and they can differ even within the same family. A sibling who values excitement might spend most of their money on outings and experiences, while another sibling who values security might rather watch a savings balance grow — same household, same rules about money, completely different spending patterns, and neither one is copying the "correct" answer from the other. As you get older and take on more responsibility — a part-time job, transportation, eventually rent — your needs list grows too, and some of today's "wants" quietly become tomorrow's "needs." The three-question test from above doesn't expire at the end of 7th grade; it's a tool you'll keep using for the rest of your life, just with bigger numbers attached each time.

Today's self-check will ask you to sort a few examples into needs and wants, and think about how your own values would show up in a spending decision.

Day 3 · Money & Emotions

NJSLS 9.1.8.FP.4
Today's goalI can identify how emotions influence spending decisions and describe a strategy for pausing before I spend.

Not every purchase is a careful decision — a lot of spending happens because of how we feel in the moment. Emotional spending shows up in a few familiar patterns: buying something because you're bored, buying something because "everyone else has it," or buying something because an ad made you feel like you'd miss out if you waited.

Does any of this sound familiar? Maybe it's opening a shopping app "just to look" on a boring afternoon and, an hour later, having added $35 of clothes to a cart you didn't plan on filling. Or it's agreeing to spend $30 on a mini-golf outing you didn't really want to go to, just because your three closest friends were all going and you didn't want to be the one who said no. In neither case is the decision really about the clothes or the mini-golf — it's about boredom in the first case, and about not wanting to feel left out in the second. That's exactly what makes emotional spending tricky to catch in the moment: it feels like it's about the item, when it's actually about the feeling underneath it.

Ads and apps are also built to trigger exactly this kind of fast, feelings-first decision on purpose. They often use urgency — "flash sale, 2 hours only," a countdown timer, "only 3 left in stock" — specifically to get you to decide before you've had time to think it over. That's not an accident; it's a marketing technique, and recognizing it for what it is makes it far easier to not fall for it every time. Companies spend enormous amounts of time and money studying exactly which words, colors, and countdown timers make people click "buy" faster — urgency and messages like "14 people are looking at this right now" are deliberately designed, not accidents. None of that makes you weak for feeling the pull; it means the pull was engineered. Recognizing the design is what lets you decide whether a purchase is really worth it to you, instead of worth it to the company selling it.

Here's why pausing actually works, with real numbers. Say you're saving for wireless headphones that cost $150, and you've committed to putting away $12 a week — an amount that's realistic because it's what you'd have left over if you skipped a $4 snack three times a week. At $12 a week, it takes $150 ÷ $12 ≈ 12.5, so about 13 weeks to reach your goal. Now imagine you start caving to that same $4 snack one extra time a week — not every day, just once more than planned. Your savings drop from $12 to $8 a week. At $8 a week, $150 ÷ $8 ≈ 18.75, so it now takes about 19 weeks instead of 13 — six extra weeks, from one small emotional purchase repeated weekly. Neither $12 nor $8 a week is a "bad" number by itself — the math simply shows how much a small, repeated habit actually costs you in time toward something you said you wanted more.

None of this means feelings don't belong in spending decisions — wanting something isn't a problem. The goal is just to notice when a decision is happening fast and give yourself a moment to catch up. One simple tool: before a purchase that isn't small, ask three questions — Do I need it? Can I afford it without giving up something else I want more? Will I still want it in a week? Even just waiting 24 hours before buying something non-urgent gives your slower, more thoughtful thinking a chance to weigh in alongside the initial excitement — which, as the headphones example shows, can be the difference between reaching a goal in 13 weeks or 19.

Try tracking it for just one week and the pattern often becomes obvious fast. Suppose you keep a simple note on your phone, and next to every purchase you jot one word: "planned" or "in-the-moment." After a week you add up the "in-the-moment" purchases and find they total $22 out of $40 spent — more than half. That's not a reason to feel bad about yourself; it's information. Once you can see the pattern written down instead of just remembering it vaguely, it gets much easier to notice which specific triggers keep showing up — is it boredom on the bus ride home, seeing a friend post about a purchase, or a particular app you open whenever you're stressed? Naming the actual trigger is usually more useful than just telling yourself to "have more willpower," because it lets you plan around the real moment instead of fighting an urge you can't quite see.

Ads aren't the only source of pressure to spend fast — sometimes it comes from friends instead of a company. Being the only person in a group who says "I'll pass" on a $15 group activity can feel uncomfortable in the moment, even when skipping it is the more sensible choice for your own goals right now. It helps to have a low-key way to say no that doesn't turn into a whole conversation — something like "I'm saving for something right now, next time for sure" usually works, and most friends respect it more than you'd expect. Just like with an ad's countdown timer, the discomfort of pausing before agreeing to spend is real, but it's temporary — and it's a much smaller cost than repeatedly saying yes to spending you didn't actually plan for.

This isn't about never buying anything you want — it's about making sure the decision is actually yours, and not just the ad's, or the group's. The more often you catch the pause working — noticing you're about to buy something out of boredom or FOMO, waiting a day, and finding the urge has faded — the easier it gets to trust the process next time, even when the countdown timer is telling you there's no time to think.

Day 4 · SMART Goals & Wrap-Up

NJSLS 9.1.8.PB.5
Today's goalI can write a SMART financial goal and explain the steps I'd take to reach it.

Over the last three days you've thought about your money mindset, what you value, and how emotions affect spending. Today those ideas come together into one practical skill: setting a goal you can actually reach.

A SMART goal is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. Compare these two goals: "I want to save up for headphones" versus "I'll save $60 for headphones by putting away $10 a week from allowance and dog-walking money for 6 weeks." The second one is Specific (headphones, $60), Measurable ($10/week), Achievable (from money you actually have coming in), Relevant (something you genuinely want), and Time-bound (6 weeks). It also does something the first version doesn't: it tells you exactly what to do this week.

Goals can be short-term (saving for a game over a month) or longer-term (saving toward a car over several years) — the SMART structure works for both. And this is where Days 1–3 matter: your mindset affects whether you believe the goal is worth trying, your values affect what you choose to save for, and your emotional-spending awareness affects whether you actually stick to the plan when something tempting comes along in week 3.

Let's build a second SMART goal from scratch so you can see exactly how each letter earns its place. Start with a weak, wishy-washy version: "I want new sneakers." That's a want, but it isn't a plan — it doesn't tell you what to actually do this week. Now SMART-ify it, one letter at a time. Specific means naming the exact thing, not a vague category — not "shoes," but a particular pair of sneakers that costs $90. Measurable means picking a number you can track — total dollars saved toward that $90. Achievable means checking that the money realistically exists: say you babysit most weekends and can set aside $15 a week without giving up something else that matters more to you. Relevant means the goal actually connects to one of your values — maybe independence, self-expression, or fitting in with people who matter to you — rather than being a random impulse. And Time-bound means doing the math to set a real deadline: $90 ÷ $15 per week = 6, so 6 weeks from today.

Put all five pieces together and the weak goal becomes: "I will save $90 for sneakers by putting away $15 a week from babysitting money for 6 weeks." That sentence tells you exactly what to do this Saturday — save $15 — while "I want new sneakers" told you nothing to do at all. That's the entire value of the SMART framework: it turns a feeling into a plan you can actually follow, one week at a time.

Before you lock in any goal, it's worth sanity-checking the "Achievable" part with simple math, because an unrealistic pace — not a lack of willpower — is the most common reason goals fail. Suppose you wanted those same $90 sneakers in just 2 weeks instead of 6: that's $90 ÷ 2 = $45 a week, which is a lot to find from babysitting or allowance in such a short window for most 7th graders. Stretching the timeline back out to 6 weeks brings it down to a far more realistic $15 a week. The item and the value behind it don't change — only the pace does. When a plan doesn't feel doable, the fix is almost never to want the thing less; it's to adjust the number of weeks until the weekly amount actually fits your real life.

Once you've written a goal, double-check it yourself by running back through all five letters like a checklist: Does it name one specific thing? Does it have a number attached? Is the weekly or monthly amount something you can realistically get? Does it connect to something you actually value? And does it have an exact deadline, not just "eventually"? If you can answer yes to all five, you've written a real plan — not just a wish.

Even a well-built SMART goal runs into real life eventually. Say partway through your six weeks toward the $90 sneakers, a friend invites you to a $20 movie night on the same week you were supposed to save your $15. Do you skip movie night, skip that one week of saving and accept a slightly longer timeline, or find an extra $5 somewhere else to cover both? None of those answers is automatically "correct" — but goals that survive real obstacles are usually the ones where you thought about a bump in the road ahead of time, instead of being caught off guard by it. That's exactly why the goal builder below asks you to name one likely obstacle and a plan for it, not just the goal itself.

Notice how everything from the last three days shows up here. Your money mindset from Day 1 affects whether you believe $15 a week is worth sticking with once it stops feeling new. Your values from Day 2 affect which goal you even choose — sneakers, savings, a gift for someone else, or something else entirely. And your emotional-spending awareness from Day 3 is exactly what helps the goal survive the week a countdown-timer sale or a spontaneous invite tries to pull that $15 somewhere else. A SMART goal isn't a replacement for those three ideas — it's the tool that turns them into a plan you can actually follow, one week at a time.

5. You're two weeks into saving $15/week toward $90 sneakers when a friend invites you to a $20 laser-tag night on your scheduled saving day. Which response best reflects the ideas from this whole unit, rather than an all-or-nothing reaction?

A SMART goal is a flexible plan, not an all-or-nothing contract — adjusting the timeline or finding a middle path uses mindset, values, and pausing-before-spending together, instead of abandoning either the goal or the friendship.
